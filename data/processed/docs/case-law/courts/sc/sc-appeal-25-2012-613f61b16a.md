Land SeƩlement – background In order to give context to the first quesƟon, it is important to understand the origins of the concept of land seƩlement. With Ceylon, as we were known at that Ɵme, becoming a colony of the BriƟsh, the system that prevailed with regard to land ownership was replaced with the PrevenƟon of Encroachments upon Crown Lands Ordinance, No. 12 of 1840. In terms of this Ordinance, all lands were declared as Crown land unƟl any claim of ownership to a parƟcular land was established by way of deeds, grants and/or such other documentary evidence. Through this process of land seƩlement arose the presumpƟon that all lands within the Country belonged to the Crown unless the contrary was established. The above Ordinance, as expected, resulted in many Ceylonese having to forgo ownership to their lands. Owing to the dissaƟsfacƟon that ensued, the Government of that Ɵme had introduced the Waste Lands Ordinance, No. 1 of 1897, in terms of which, all unculƟvated lands were declared as Crown land. The harsh manner in which the new law was implemented and the injusƟce caused to the People by the said law has been well documented and was even the subject maƩer of debate in the House of Lords where Lord Stanley of Alderly had implored upon “the necessity of an independent inquiry into the working of the Ceylon Waste Lands Ordinance, and to the injusƟce caused ...”. [Debates of 17th July 1899 - hƩps://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/lords/1899/jul/17/ceylon￾waste-lands-ordinance] The indiscriminate manner in which the presumpƟon that all unculƟvated lands are State lands was applied in pracƟce was referred to by Chief JusƟce Bertram in Hamina Etena v The Assistant Government Agent, PuƩalam [23 NLR 289; at page 290], when he stated that, “According to that presumpƟon, all forests, waste, unoccupied, or unculƟvated lands, and all chenas are presumed to be the property of the Crown unless the contrary thereof is proved. It is hardly contested that, if the presumpƟon applies, the evidence adduced on behalf of the complainant is not sufficient to displace it.” 9 The Land SeƩlement Ordinance – the provisions It is in this background that the Land SeƩlement Ordinance was introduced for the seƩlement of lands. The scheme laid down in the Ordinance comprised of three stages, they being (1) the publicaƟon of a SeƩlement NoƟce specifying the lands that the State wished to seƩle, (2) the acceptance of claims in respect of the lands referred to in such SeƩlement NoƟce, and an inquiry into such claims, and finally (3) the making of a SeƩlement Order followed by its publicaƟon in the GazeƩe. I shall now consider these three stages in detail. In terms of SecƟon 4(1), whenever it appears to the SeƩlement Officer that any land (a) is forest, waste, unoccupied, or unculƟvated land, or (b) is culƟvated or otherwise improved land but was forest, waste, unoccupied, or unculƟvated land in the preceding twenty five years, he may publish a SeƩlement NoƟce containing details of such lands and specifying that, “if no claim to such land or to any share of or interest in such land is made to him within a period of three months from a date to be specified in such noƟce the land to which or to any share of or interest in which no claim has been made as aforesaid will be declared under SecƟon 5 (1) to be the property of the State and will be dealt with on account of the State.” In terms of SecƟon 5(1), “If no claim is made within a period of three months ..., the SeƩlement Officer shall make a declaraƟon in wriƟng, which shall be deemed for the purposes of this Ordinance to be a seƩlement in favour of the State, that such land to which or to any share of or interest in which no claim has been made is the property of the State”. If however a claim is made within such period, SecƟon 5(2) requires the SeƩlement Officer to proceed to hold an inquiry into such claim. At the conclusion of the inquiry, the SeƩlement Officer may do one of the following specified in SecƟon 5(4): (a) Make a declaraƟon in wriƟng [which shall be deemed to be a seƩlement] that any land specified in the SeƩlement NoƟce is not claimed by the State; 10 (b) Make a declaraƟon in wriƟng [which shall be deemed to be a seƩlement] that some person unascertained is enƟtled to a parƟcular share of or interest in any land specified in the SeƩlement NoƟce; (c) “Enter with the claimant, upon such terms and condiƟons as may appear fit to the SeƩlement Officer, into an agreement in wriƟng signed by the SeƩlement Officer and by the claimant, providing ..., that the said claimant ... shall be declared by SeƩlement Order under subsecƟon (5) to be enƟtled either wholly or in part, ... to any land or to any share of or interest in any land specified in the seƩlement noƟce, and make a seƩlement of such land or share or interest in pursuance of such agreement:” SecƟon 5(5) requires the SeƩlement Officer to thereaŌer embody such seƩlement under (a), (b) or (c) above in a SeƩlement Order. The process of land seƩlement culminates with SecƟon 8 in terms of which: “... every seƩlement order shall be published in the GazeƩe, and every seƩlement order so published shall be judicially noƟced and shall be conclusive proof, so far as the State or any person is thereby declared to be enƟtled to any land or to any share of or interest in any land, that the State or such person is enƟtled to such land or to such share of or interest in the land free of all encumbrances whatsoever other than those specified in such order and that subject to the encumbrances specified in such order such land or share or interest vests absolutely ... in such person to the exclusion of all unspecified interests of whatsoever nature ...”. Thus, the scheme laid down in the Ordinance seeks to achieve two objecƟves. The first is to idenƟfy lands that belong to private parƟes within the area specified in the SeƩlement NoƟce and for the State to recognise the interests and/or enƟtlement of such persons to a parƟcular land referred to in such NoƟce. The second is to determine and declare the lands that belong to the State which are situated within such SeƩlement NoƟce. Once this process is completed and the SeƩlement Order is published in the GazeƩe, such Order becomes conclusive proof of the status of the lands referred to in the Order and wipes out all encumbrances that existed upto that point in respect of such lands. This is reflected in the sƟpulaƟon in SecƟon 8 that a SeƩlement Order shall be conclusive proof that the 11 State or such person is enƟtled to such land free of all encumbrances whatsoever other than those specified in such order and that the land shall vest absolutely in such person or the State, as the case may be. This secƟon, inter alia, provides that a SeƩlement Officer may enter into an agreement with a claimant whereby a claimant or any other person shall be declared by SeƩlement Order under sub-secƟon (5) of the secƟon to be enƟtled either wholly or in part to any land specified in the SeƩlement NoƟce [subsecƟon (4)(c)] and sub-secƟon (5) says that the SeƩlement Officer shall embody any such seƩlement in a SeƩlement Order which shall be substanƟally as set out in Form No. 2 in the First Schedule. That seems to me to make it reasonably certain that the Legislature meant Form No. 2 to be declaratory for proceedings iniƟated under the Land SeƩlement Ordinance” [Page 86] “Under secƟon 8 it is the SeƩlement Order “so published”, that is in the Ceylon Government GazeƩe, that shall be judicially noted as conclusive proof of Ɵtle. Can it be said that Ɵtle passes from the Crown at the date of the agreement although it is the published SeƩlement Order which ulƟmately provides the Ɵtle holder with conclusive proof of his Ɵtle? 15 I do not think that the wording of sub-secƟon (4) (c) of secƟon 5 of the Land SeƩlement Ordinance supports such a view. The essence of the agreement is the undertaking by the Crown to seƩle land subsequently on the claimant by the procedure provided for by sub-secƟon (5 ).“ [page 86-87; emphasis added] Thus, the enƟtlement of a claimant to a parƟcular land which is recognised through an agreement is formalised by the publicaƟon of a SeƩlement Order, vesƟng such land absolutely in such claimant, with such Order receiving the legal sancƟty referred to in SecƟon 8. In Periacaruppen Cheƫar v Messrs Proprietors and Agents Ltd and others [(1946) 47 NLR 121; at page 125] Chief JusƟce Howard, having considered the precise effect of an order under SecƟon 8 of the Land SeƩlement Ordinance stated that, “In my opinion the order of October 27, 1933, is conclusive proof of the Ɵtle of Gunasekera. The order vests the Ɵtle in Gunasekera subject to any encumbrances specified in the order. All unspecified interests are excluded. ... The seƩlement order, therefore, wiped out and destroyed all previous Ɵtles to the property and vested it in Gunasekera.” [emphasis added] Thus, Chief JusƟce Howard proceeded on the basis that a deed executed prior to the SeƩlement Order cannot be classified as an encumbrance since no reference has been made in the SeƩlement Order to the said deed and therefore accepted the posiƟon laid down in SecƟon 8 that the effect of the publicaƟon of a SeƩlement Order is that it shall be conclusive proof that the person referred to in a SeƩlement Order is enƟtled to such land free of all encumbrances whatsoever other than those specified in such order. While SecƟon 8 is clear with regard to the effect of a SeƩlement Order, what a SeƩlement Order does is twofold. The first is, there is recogniƟon by the State that the private party who claimed the land pursuant to the publicaƟon of a SeƩlement NoƟce has an enƟtlement in respect of such land and for that reason, declares that the State has no claim over such land. The second is a SeƩlement Order confers conclusive proof that such person is enƟtled to such land free of all encumbrances whatsoever other than those specified in such order, and that the said land shall vest absolutely in such person. 16 In this case, pursuant to the SeƩlement NoƟce being published, Agreement P7 was executed on 23rd July 1954 which recognised and declared that Podi Appuhamy is enƟtled to 1A 3R 3P in Sub Lot No. 268 in Lot No. 85 in Village Plan No. 888. Thus, the interest of Podi Appuhami to the said land has been recognised as far back as 1954, and the SeƩlement Order published in 1996 is a formal recogniƟon of the enƟtlement of Podi Appuhamy to such land to the exclusion of all others including the State. The SeƩlement Order P1 was published 43 years aŌer the publicaƟon of the SeƩlement NoƟce specifying the names of the persons on whom lands referred to in Village Plan No. 888 have been seƩled. The claim of Podi Appuhamy has been duly recognised in 1954, the land has been seƩled in his favour in 1993, and there is formal recogniƟon by the publicaƟon of P1 in 1996 that Ɵtle has vested absolutely in Podi Appuhamy. As expected, most of the persons on whom lands have been seƩled since the publicaƟon of the SeƩlement NoƟce in 1953, including Podi Appuhamy had passed away by the Ɵme P1 was published. Keeping in mind the objecƟve that is sought to be achieved by the system of land seƩlement that I have referred to, I am of the view that Podi Appuhamy, and since he had passed away by the Ɵme of the publicaƟon of the SeƩlement Order, his heirs, are enƟtled to the land referred to in P1. The death of Podi Appuhamy does not viƟate his Ɵtle to the said land nor does the land ‘revert’ to the State but the land shall devolve on his heirs and they shall be enƟtled to claim such land. To say that the right of the heirs of Podi Appuhamy to claim the benefit of such seƩlement has been exƟnguished by the passing away of Podi Appuhamy does not stand to reason. Accordingly, the PlainƟff, being a son of Podi Appuhamy is enƟtled to a share of such land upon the death of Podi Appuhamy. The High Court stated that: “P7 goes to show that a decision has been taken to seƩle the land in favour of Podi Appuhami in that both Lots 85 and 268 have been menƟoned. It is clear by perusing P1 that what has been seƩled in favour of Podi Appuhamy is Sub division No. 268 of Lot No. 85, which has been registered at the Land Registry in Volume No. G136 and Folio No. 180. It is also significant to note that in the GazeƩe P1, it is menƟoned that SeƩlement NoƟce under SecƟon 4 of the Land SeƩlement Ordinance was duly published in the GazeƩe No. 10526 of 15th May 1953 and all claims received in 17 pursuance of the said NoƟce were duly dealt with prior to making that SeƩlement Order. Hence, it is abundantly clear that despite the fact that the SeƩlement Order has been published aŌer the death of Podi Appuhamy the maƩer had been set in moƟon at least since 1953 when Podi Appuhamy was very much alive. Therefore it is apparent that the provisional decision reflected in P7 is a consequenƟal step taken aŌer the publicaƟon of the SeƩlement NoƟce in 1953.” I am in agreement with the view taken by the High Court. The seƩlement process that commenced in 1953 culminated in 1996. The fact that Podi Appuhamy passed away prior to the publicaƟon of the SeƩlement Order does not make P1 a nullity nor does the land revert to the State. Instead, his Ɵtle devolves on his heirs. In these circumstances, I am of the view that the PlainƟff was enƟtled to file a parƟƟon acƟon. Conclusion In the above circumstances, I answer the quesƟons of law Nos. 1, 3 and 4 in the negaƟve. The necessity to consider quesƟon of law No. 2 does not arise. The judgments of the District Court and the High Court are hereby affirmed and this appeal is accordingly dismissed, without costs.
