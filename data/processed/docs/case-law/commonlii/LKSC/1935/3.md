IN THE MATTER OF A RULE UNDER SECTION 51 OF THE COURTS ORDINANCE, NO. 1 OF 1889, ON ALEXANDER FRANCIS MOLAMURE EXECUTOR OF THE LAST WILL AND TESTAMENT OF THE LATE J. W. MADUWANWALA. Contempt of Court-Disobedience of order of the District Court-Probate issued to executor-Permission to draw money from bank leaving a balance for estate duty-Defiance of order-Courts Ordinance, No. 1 of 1889, ss. 51 and 59. Where a District Court issued probate to an executor and allowed him to withdraw from the bank a certain sum of money, lying to the credit of the estate leaving a balance, which was intended to defray the cost of estate duty. Held , that disobedience of the order of the District Court amounted to contempt of its authority. Held, further , that in the circumstances the Supreme Court alone had jurisdiction to take cognizance of and punish such a contempt. THIS was a rule calling on the respondent to show cause why he X should not be dealt with under the provisions of section 51 of the Courts Ordinance for contempt of the authority of the District Court of Ratnapura in that he, being the duly appointed executor of a last will proved in that Court, and being prohibited by an order of July 29, 1931, from drawing, without permission of the Court, money lying in deposit in the bank, did withdraw from the bank a sum of Rs. 52,000 when permission had been granted to him to withdraw only a sum of Rs. 22,000. F. J. Soertsz, K.C. (with him C. V. Ranawake), for respondent.-The question of contempt raised in this rule should be tried by the District Court. Section 51 of the Courts Ordinance provides that the Supreme Court shall have full power and authority to try any offence of contempt against or in disrespect of the authority of itself, or any offence of contempt against or in disrespect of any other Court and which such Court has not jurisdiction under section 59 to take cognizance of or punish. In this instance, it is submitted that the District Court of Ratnapura has jurisdiction, as the alleged offence of contempt was an offence committed in the course of any act or proceeding in the said Court. It was an offence committed in the course of the testamentary proceedings in the District Court of Ratnapura. The local decisions on the point are wrong and should be reconsidered. The rule should be discharged as the offence imputed is a disregard of an order said to have been made on July 29, and it is now clear that there was no order on that date. If, however, one relies on the order of August 4 as also implied in the offence imputed as a contempt against the respondent, it is submitted that that was an order made without jurisdiction and therefore not binding on the respondent. (a) It is not an order made irregularly or wrongly by a Court having jurisdiction on, but an order made by a Court which had no jurisdiction to make such an order. 34 (b) An executor, such as the respondent, gets his authority from the probate and that authority cannot in any way he fettered, or diminished by an order of Court; for a Court to attempt to do so, would amount to an attempt to make a different will than the testator had made. (c) Section 23 (4) of Ordinance No. 8 of 1919 does not alter the position. Even assuming a Court has power to say that probate will be issued on certain conditions being fulfilled by the executor and to withhold issue of probate pending fulfilment, once probate did issue or reached the executor's hands, he had all the power the will gave him. On the facts, it is submitted that the order of July 29 was not communicated to the respondent; it was not an order but a minute for the guidance of the Court. Even if it was an order, it was an order applicable to the state of things in existence at the time it was made, namely, when without obtaining probate, the executor was seeking the permission of Court for drawing a sum of money to enable him to make certain payments. It ceased to have any effect once probate was issued to him. The money in question was the executor's money by virtue of the will -it was money bequeathed to him. If it was not his money exclusively by operation of the will, it became his money once the settlement between himself and the other heir was reached. There were vast tracts of land available for the securing of the payment of estate duty. The Commissioner of Stamps could not insist on the estate duty being paid in cash, It was at the option of the executor to ask to pay by instalments and to hypothecate lands for the securing of the payments. The respondent gave no undertaking after probate had been issued to him, even if it be assumed that he gave such an undertaking before. J. W. R. Ilangakoon, S.-G. (with him Basnayake, C.C., and Pulle, C.C.), as amicus curiae.-It is the Supreme Court and not the District Court that has jurisdiction in this matter. The District Court could not have taken proceedings for contempt in view of the language of section 59 of the Courts Ordinance. There is no law in force which makes the offence of contempt under consideration punishable by the District Court. The Civil Procedure Code makes no provision for a case such as this.- That the Supreme Court has jurisdiction in a case such as this is concluded by the decisions of Annamalay Chetty v. Gunaratne [ 1 1 N. L. R. 49.] and The King v. Samaraweera [ 2 19 N. L. R. 433.] These are decisions of the Full Bench. The order of the Court prohibiting the withdrawal of the money in the bank without the permission of Court was intra vires of its powers. It was competent to make the order and the respondent was bound to obey it so long as it stood undischarged (Silva v. Appuhamy[ 3 4 N. L R. 178.] ). If an order is improper or irregular the remedy of the party affected thereby is not to disobey it but to take proper steps to have it set aside. If a party disobeys an order which a Court has jurisdiction to make, he does so at his peril especially when the party affected thereby has expressly acquiesced in the order and acted in accordance with it. 35 He has further given an undertaking to leave a stated balance sum of money in the bank. This undertaking has been broken. It is contempt of Court to break an undertaking given to a Court-7 Hals. 2nd ed., p. 31. The respondent knew of the order of July 29. The Secretary's evidence and his subsequent conduct prove it. It is not a mere minute made by the Secretary for his guidance but it is an order of Court. The probate issued subsequently was subject to this order. Otherwise the respondent would not have applied for permission to withdraw a sum of Rs. 20,00ft out of the money in the bank. The fact that he concealed the existence of the order of Court from the manager shows that he know the order and its effect. The money was money that was meant to be utilized for payment of the testamentary expenses. Even according to the agreement of parties the respondent was not free to utilize the money as he wished. It was set apart for a definite purpose. Counsel cited the following cases:-Fennings v. Humphrey[ 1 4 Beavan 1.] In re Batteshy's Estate[ 2 (1892) 31 L. R. 73.]; Callow v. Young[ 3 SS. L. 544.]; Blake v. Blake[ 4 Bead. 514.]; In re Witten[ 5 4 Times L. R. 36.]. Cur. adv. vult. February 18,1935. MACDONELL C.J.- This was a rule calling on Alexander Francis Molamure to show cause why he should not be dealt with under the provisions of section 51 of the Courts Ordinance for contempt of the authority of the District Court of Ratnapura in that he, being the duly appointed executor of a will proved in that Court in testamentary case No. 971, and being prohibited by an order of July 29, 1931, from drawing, without permission of the Court, money lying in deposit in the bank, did withdrawn from the bank a sum of Rs. 52,000 when permission had been granted him to withdrawn only a sum of Rs. 22,000. Mr. Maduwanwala died on September 6, 1930, leaving a last will which appointed the respondent to this rule, sole executor. By clause 2 it gave a life interest in all property, movable and immovable, to-the widow and daughter of the testator. The estate was to be managed by the respondent to this rule who was a nephew of the testator, and the life tenants (widow and daughter) were prohibited from any dealings with the movables and immovables in which they were thus given a life interest. Clause 3 provides that after the decease of the widow and daughter, all the immovable property was to " devolve" on the respondent for a half share, each of the remaining two quarters being bequeathed to certain beneficiaries, all of them minors. By clause 8, all movable property was to " devolve " on the respondent who was not however to alienate jewellery held as family heirlooms. Clause 10 gave the village of Maduwanwala exclusively to the respondent subject to the life interest in the widow and daughter, and clause 12 appointed the respondent sole executor of the will. In the event, there came to be a difference of opinion between the beneficiaries under this will as to its effect. The widow and daughter and the person eventually appointed guardian of the minor beneficiaries claimed that under clause 2 of the will they (the widow and daughter) had a life interest in all money lying to the credit of the testator 36 as being movable property. The executor, the respondent to this rule, claimed that under clause 8 the money, being a movable, came directly to him without an intervening life interest. It would be possible to argue that the phraseology of clause 8, namely, that all the movable property was to " devolve" on the executor, the respondent to this rule, meant, after the life interest created by clause 2. The property bequeathed was made subject to fidei commissum. On March 16, 1931, the respondent-executor, through his proctors Messrs. Attygalle & Muttettuwegama, applied for probate in the District Court of Ratnapura in testamentary action No. 971, filing the usual petition to which the life tenants, the minor beneficiaries and their guardian, were made respondents, and with that petition the usual schedule of the property bequeathed. The immovable property which consisted of large tracts of land in various places was shown in the schedule as of the value of Rs. 303,340, and the movables as of the value of Rs. 77,396.17, total value Rs. 380,736. The movables included cash in the Imperial Bank Rs. 52,000. Rule nisi ,was obtained on March 16, 1931, returnable on May 7, thereafter extended to June 5. As has been said, there was a difference of opinion between the persons interested as to the construction of this will and this difference centred round the movables of the estate. The " widow and daughter, first and second respondents, supported by the sixth respondent;, guardian of two of the minor beneficiaries, evidently claimed that their life interest extended to the -movables. The executor, respondent to this rule, claimed that by clause 8 the movables came to him absolutely, as has been said. The return date of the rule nisi was extended on various dates, and on July 6, the proctor for the first, second, and sixth respondents stated in the Court at Ratnapura that he had no objection to probate being granted. The matter was then fixed for August 5. On July 26 there was a meeting at the Ratnapura Resthouse at which were present the executor, respondent to this rule, the sixth respondent himself with a power of attorney from the first and second respondents, the proctor for those parties, Mr. Attygalle the executor's proctor, and certain other persons who do not need to be mentioned. After discussion, a settlement was arrived at, written out in Sinhalese. Some of its clauses were designed (we were told) to enable the life tenants to have the management as well as the enjoyment of the property in which they were liferented, and the respondent to this rule also said that these clauses were inserted because the life tenants objected to him managing the properties by means of his servants; they wished to manage them by their own servants. There follow two clauses, 6 and 7, the translation of which made for us by the Court Mudaliyar is as follows: "6. Excluding completely the afore-mentioned villages and lands in the Kolonna korale belonging to the said estate the party of the first part (i.e., the executor) has reserved for himself the right and the liberty to enjoy all the remaining movable and immovable properties in whatever manner he pleases. " 7. The moneys to be paid to Government in respect of the estate, the expenses incurred in connection with the testamentary case, and all debts if any due and owing from Maduwanwala Bandara (the 37 testator) shall be paid by the party of the first part (i.e., the executor). Further, out of the money deposited in the bank by the said testator, whether Maduwanwala Kumarihamy (i.e., the daughter) has any claim thereto or not, the party of the first part (i.e., the executor) shall pay unto the said daughter a sum of Rupees One thousand within a month from the date of the party of the first part (i.e., the executor) obtaining probate ". The executor, respondent to this rule, undertakes then to pay whatever may be due to Government on the estate-this would include estate duties-and the debts left by the testator, and he also promises ex gratia, to pay one of the life tenants the sum of Rs. 1,000 within one month after obtaining probate. In return the life tenants withdraw their claim to a life interest in the movables including the cash lying in the bank. This settlement was filed in Court in the testamentary case No. 971 on September 7, 1931. The executor, respondent to this rule, took the settlement to the Government Agent, obtained his approval of it, and seems to have left Ratnapura the same day, July 26, 1931. He says that before leaving he instructed his proctors to " take the next steps necessary", also to put in a motion to draw the money, and that he would be in Ratnapura again on August 3. He had already written in April to his proctors saying that he would want Rs. 5,000 and he informed us that at that time (July 26) he had already received claims against the estate amounting to Rs. 8,000, mainly debts to different shops. Alms-giving, funeral expenses, and upkeep of the different residences of the deceased would, he said, bring his requirements to some Rs. 12,000 to Rs. 15,000. It was for this reason that he instructed his proctors to put in a motion enabling him to draw money, he says, to the amount of Rs. 22,000. There is no evidence that he was in Ratnapura again till August 4. On July 29, three days that is after this meeting at which the settlement was arrived at, his proctors put in the following motion : -" We file letter of consent from the third respondent dated July 26, 1931, together with letter from his proctor dated the 29th, and move that probate be issued to the executor." This motion was received by the Chief Clerk of the Court and sent to the Secretary in the usual manner. The Secretary endorsed upon it is follows:-"Issue probate after the oath is filed. Money in the Imperial Bank should not be drawn without permission of Court", and it was duly signed by the then District Judge. It was strenuously maintained by the respondent to this rule that he had at no time knowledge of this motion of July 29, or of the order made there under, and in confirmation he pointed to the fact that there was nothing in the letter book of his proctors to show that they communicated with him at any time after the meeting of July 26 until he next came to Ratnapura on August 4. On August 3, he telegraphed to his proctors in Ratnapura, "Not coming today-will be there to-morrow-please have papers ready". The next calling day for the case was August 5, it may be noticed. If the respondent knew of the motion of July 29 asking for probate, then the " papers" to be ready would be the oath of office and inventory 38 The respondent denies knowledge of this motion saying that all he had asked his proctors to do was to prepare a motion for drawing the money, and he did not, I think, specify to us what these " papers " were. There is again a conflict as to what happened on August 4. His proctors say that he called on them at their office before going to Court. The respondent denies that he went there or saw them there and says that he went straight to the Court without going to their office at all. He says he had come that day from Kegalla, a distance of 60 miles, and that he could not have arrived in Ratnapura before 9.15 or 9.30. Mr. Attygalle was asked in cross-examination at what hour he saw respondent that morning, but it was not put either to him or his partner that the respondent denied coming to their office at all that day, and it came somewhat as a surprise when the respondent giving evidence denied he went to their office or saw them there. Mr. Attygalle is quite clear that the respondent did come to him there and that they discussed together in his office the motion of July 29 and the Court's order thereon, namely, that probate could be issued but that the money was not to be withdrawn without an order of Court. He says further that the respondent discussed with him the amount that he would need from the money in the bank, that the sum of Rs. 22,000 was agreed upon and that it was decided to leave Rs. 30,000 in the Bank, he says, to meet estate duty. At this time no assessment of estate duty had been made by the Commissioner of Stamps. In any case the respondent's proctors did put in that day the following motion: -" We move that the Court be pleased to grant the executor permission to draw a sum of Rs. 22,000 from and out of the sum of Rs. 52,000 lying in deposit in the Imperial Bank to the credit of the deceased's estate and that the balance sum of Rs. 30,000 do remain to the credit of the case ". This motion was taken to Court, and the two Court officials concerned, the Chief Clerk and the Secretary were minutely examined and cross-examined as to what was done with it. It is admitted that with it there was put in the oath of office which the executor would have to swear to before obtaining probate, and that the Court officials drew up the probate for submission to the Court. The Chief Clerk says himself that he made two copies of the probate at the request of the Secretary, one coy of which was sent to the District Judge for signature and that the other copy was certified by the Secretary and handed to the respondent. On the motion itself the Secretary made the following journal entry, " Proctors for the executor file oath of office and inventory and move that the Court be pleased to grant the executor .permission to draw the sum of Rs. 22,000 out of the sum of Rs. 52,000 lying in deposit in the Imperial Bank to the credit of the deceased's estate, leaving a balance of Rs. 30,000 to the credit of the estate" and after consideration, he wrote, " allowed" below this journal entry. The papers, namely, probate, oath of office, and journal entry were then taken to the Judge and duly executed ; that is to say, the respondent affirmed the oath of office and the Judge signed the probate and handed it to the respondent from the Bench; he also initialled, then or earlier, the "allowance" of the motion embodied in the journal entry. After that the respondent seems to have gone back to the Secretary's office and obtained what he had asked for previously, namely, a copy of the 39 journal entry of that date which, having been allowed by the Judge, permitted him to draw Rs. 22,000 from the Bank leaving a balance of Rs. 30,000 to the credit of the case. It is important here to examine the conflict of testimony between respondent and his proctors, or rather Mr. Attygalle who was taking a bigger share in the matter than his partner. Mr. Attygalle says that the motion of July 29 was put in on respondents instructions and that the sum to be drawn, Rs. 22,000, was only settled at their talk with him on August 4, also that he knew of the order of July 29 before he again saw respondent on August 4, and the Chief Clerk, Mr. Kodituwaku, bears him out since he (the Chief Clerk) made entries in his register that respondent's proctors had applied for the record of the case on July 29 and again on August 3. Mr. Attygalle knew then of the order of July 29 on his motion of that date before he again saw respondent on August 4. Respondent denies all knowledge of this motion of July 29 or order thereon, or that he ever gave instructions for that motion, and says that he had already on July 26 told his proctors that he wanted to draw money specifying the amount, Rs. 22,000. It is difficult to believe that proctors of the experience of these people, or indeed that any men of business, should have so totally disregarded their instructions as to ask on July 29 for probate and to omit all mention of respondent's desire to draw Rs. 22,000, to move for something respondent had not asked them to get him and to omit the one thing he had asked them to get. They certainly were culpably lax in not having paid regard to section 23 (4) of Ordinance No. 8 of 1919, which says- " (4) No probate or letters of administration shall issue from a District Court unless and until there has been filed in such Court a certificate certifying that the executor has paid or secured to the satisfaction of the Commissioner of Stamps the payment of all estate duty for which he is accountable." It was there duty in moving for probate to have brought this section clearly to the notice of the Secretary and the District Judge, but these considerations do not touch the question, which is the more probable story as to this motion of July 29 and order thereon. The proctors say they discussed these with respondent on August 4. Respondent denies any such discussion, and studiously minimizes the amount of communication that he had with his proctors that day at all. Yet how on his story are we to frame an intelligible account of what happened that day? On arrival, whether at his proctor's office or at the Court, he would have to ask someone, one of his proctors or the Secretary of the Court, is my order to draw Rs. 22,000 ready. Respondent himself puts it this way, "I went and sat next to Mr. Attygalle at the table . . . . I spoke to him and asked him " what had been done ". How could Mr. Attygalle give an answer that would be intelligible to the respondent without mentioning the order of July 29? Even if respondent on seeing the motion of :August 4, or on obtaining at his own request a certified copy of the order thereon, made no remark as to the Rs. 30,000 to be retained -his own story is, that all he had asked them to procure him was an order to draw Rs. 22,000-still it is inconceivable that someone would not 40 have told him that this reservation as to the Rs. 30,000 was because of the order of July 29 which prohibited any money being withdrawn at all without permission of the Court; that it was a modification in his favour of that order. The respondent when asked why his proctors should invent an interview and conversation that had never occurred said that they did so to minimize their culpability as to Ordinance No. 8 of 1919, section 23 (4) but it is difficult to see how their telling him of the order of July 29 does this. By pushing through the order of August 4 they left estate duty less secured than under the order of July 29, which prohibited money being drawn without order of Court and so implicitly did provide for estate duty. Their telling him of the order of July 29, as they say they did, seems to make their position rather worse than better. The evidence of the Chief Clerk, Mr. Kodituwaku, and of the Secretary of the Court, Mr. Waidiyaratne, likewise affects the respondent with knowledge of the order of July 29. The Chief Clerk says that the respondent was seated at the Secretary's table when the Secretary referred to the journal entry of July 29, and he also says that the respondent referred to the entry himself. The Secretary himself says that it was on his responsibility that the prohibition against drawing money without the permission of Court was inserted in the order of July 29, and adds: "I explained things to Mr. Molamure, i.e., about leaving the money in the bank, and he said he would make arrangements to pay by instalments and until then he would not draw the money ", and further on he says as to the motion of August 4: "It is this motion that was allowed by me in the presence of Mr. Molamure. We discussed the matter and I allowed the motion-he was seated at my table opposite to me ". Again he says: " I read (the motion) that the executor was going to leave Rs. 30,000 to the credit of the case. I do not remember anyone telling me but I told Mr. Molamure and he agreed. He said he would bring an authority from the Commissioner of Stamps and pay by instalments and later draw the money. I then suggested on the application that it be allowed . . . . I remember telling him that that money was reserved for estate duty ". Again, there was culpable laxity on the part of this Court official in not seeing that estate duty was more adequately secured, but it is difficult to see how he lessens to any appreciable extent the blame on himself by saying that he brought to the notice of the respondent the order of July 29. Did he discuss the motion of August 4 with the respondent ? The respondent denies that he did. But again it is difficult to frame an intelligible account of the events of that August 4 without some discussion having taken place between the Secretary and the respondent, and if a discussion did take place at all, the order of July 29 must have been referred to in it. The evidence both of the Chief Clerk and of the Secretary was vague in places, as might be expected after the lapse of over three years. Although the Secretary was aware that he had been lax, his evidence did not, however, sound like that of a man attempting to deny his mistakes, still less of one trying to put the blame on somebody else. On the contrary he admitted that he had been wrong. The probabilities of the matter are overwhelmingly in favour of his version-supported in part by the evidence of the Chief Clerk-and 41 of that of the respondent's proctors being correct as to this that the respondent, at any rate on August 4, was made perfectly aware of the motion of July 29 and of the order thereon. It should be observed that the Chief Clerk says he knew that probate could not issue without a certificate from the Commissioner of Stamps, though the Secretary professed himself less well versed in the requirements of Ordinance No. 8 of 1919. The Chief Clerk stated to us that a certificate from the Commissioner of Stamps had always been filed before the issue of probate in every testamentary case that had come within his knowledge in the District Court of Ratnapura, save in one where money was actually lying in the Kachcheri to the credit of the estate sufficient to secure payment of estate duty. The Secretary stated that he knew that estate duty had to be paid though he did not know at what stage. The fact that the Secretary of his own act put into the order of July 29 the prohibition on drawing money without leave of the Court can only be explained on the supposition that he was thinking of estate duty, and he says that his object was to reserve the money in the bank to pay that duty, and the District Judge said that he was informed in Court that the Rs. 30,000 was reserved for this purpose. The persons responsible were lax but they had not altogether forgotten the requirements of the law as to estate duty. The credibility of the respondent as a witness will be discussed later, but it will be convenient at this point to give certain findings as to the facts. We are satisfied that the respondent had instructed Messrs. Attygalle & Muttettuwegama to put in the motion of July 29 and that the proctors and Court officials are correct when they say that on August 4 they and the respondent discussed the order of July 29, as also the motion of August 4 itself and the order thereon that Rs. 30,000 was reserved, so as to secure estate duty. We are satisfied that the respondent acquiesced in that reservation and undertook not to draw that sum and that everyone concerned-proctors, Court officials, and District Judge- relied (most unfortunately) on that undertaking by the respondent. We are also satisfied that the respondent's statement that he never looked on the words " leaving a balance of Rs. 30,000 to the credit of the case" as a prohibition against drawing that amount, is untrue. It is important to realize the position in which the respondent, the executor of this estate, now stood. He had received the document called probate, giving him full authority as executor over the estate and its assets. He had also received, indeed he had asked for this, a copy of the journal entry of that date giving him permission to withdraw Rs. 22,000 leaving a balance of Rs. 30,000 to the credit of the estate. These two documents really contradicted each other. If the executor had probate, that by itself allowed him to draw all the money then in the bank. If his authority to draw the money from the bank was to be limited, as the journal entry of that date did limit it, then logically he ought not to have received probate at all. The two documents were not connected in any way. There was no reference in either one to the other. It was therefore within the power of the holder to make use of either or both. In this state of things a letter should have been sent from the Court to the bank acquainting it with the order that Rs. 30,000 was to be 42 left to the credit of the case. This was not done. The District Judge who was new to his duties thought that the Court Secretary would do something of the sort. The Court Secretary says he had no instructions to write anything to the bank, so did not write anything to it. At this stage it will be convenient to examine the legal effect of these orders of the Court and of the undertaking given by the respondent on August 4. But first we will discuss and answer certain preliminary objections raised on behalf of the respondent. The first of these was that the proper Court to try the contempt charged here was the District Court of Ratnapura and not this Court. The statutory provisions on the question are to be found in sections 51 and 59 of the Courts Ordinance. Section 51 reads as follows :- "51. The Supreme Court or any Judge thereof, whether at Colombo or elsewhere, shall have full power and authority to take cognizance of and to try in a summary manner any offence of contempt committed against or in disrespect of the authority of itself or any offence of contempt committed against or in disrespect of the authority of any other Court, and which such Court has not jurisdiction under section 59 to take cognizance of and punish . . . . " The contempt alleged here was committed " against or in disrespect of the authority of another Court" and if that Court has no jurisdiction under section 59 to take cognizance of and punish such a contempt, then clearly it is this Court which is empowered to do so. Section 59 reads as follows :- " 59. Every District Court, Court of Requests, and Police Court shall, for the purpose of maintaining its proper authority and efficiency, have a special jurisdiction to take cognizance of, and to punish by the procedure and with the penalties in that behalf by law provided, every offence of contempt of Court committed in the presence of the Court itself, and all offences which are committed in the course of any act or proceeding in the said Courts respectively, and which are declared by any law for the time being in force to be punishable as contempts of Court." Instances of conduct declared by law to be contempt of Court and punishable as such will be found in the Civil Procedure Code, sections 137, 294, 295, 358, 650, 656, 682, 713, 717, and 718. Now, section 59 has received the same interpretation in two Full Bench decisions (Annamalay Chetty v. Gunaratne [ 1 N. L. R. 49.] and The King v. Samaraweera [ 2 19 N. L. R. 433.] ), which decisions are binding upon us. We would refer to the discussion of this section 59 by Wood Renton C.J. in the latter of the two cases cited, at 19 N. L. R. 434. After quoting section 59 in full he proceeds to say, " This section is obviously capable of two different constructions. It may mean either that the District Court may punish as contempt offences in facie curiae, or offences (a) committed in the course of an act or proceeding in the District Court which (b) are declared to be so punishable by any law for the time being in force ; or that the punitive powers in the District Court extend to offences (a) in facie curiae, or (b) committed in the course of any act or proceeding in the District Court, or (c) declared punishable 43 by the District Court by any law for the time being in force. After careful consideration of the language of section 59, I think that it should be construed in the former sense. The fact that the section provides that District Courts and Courts of Requests shall be Courts of record does not show that the legislature intended to confer upon them unlimited jurisdiction in matters of this kind. The County Courts in England have been made Courts of Record by statute, but their jurisdiction to punish for contempt does not extend to acts done ex facie curiae. But the point appears to me to be really covered by the decision of the full Court in Annamalay Chetty v. Gunaratne (supra) in which section 59 of the Courts Ordinance, 1889, was expressly construed in this sense. " In this interpretation of the section Shaw and Ennis JJ. concurred, and it is an interpretation of the section which is binding upon us. Since there is no law in force declaring such a contempt as this to be an " offence committed in the course of an act or proceeding " in a District Court, and " punishable " accordingly " as a contempt", it follows that a District Court has no jurisdiction over such a contempt as the one now alleged, and it also follows by section 51 that the Supreme Court has jurisdiction over such a contempt and that it is the only Court that has that jurisdiction. If, however, we understood learned Counsel for the respondent correctly, he invited us, instead of applying the two decisions just referred to, to apply certain " Common law principles ", the nature of which however he did not discuss. If we refer to the case of In re John Ferguson[ 1 1 N. L. R. 181.], decided in 1874 and therefore before the enactment of the Courts Ordinance, we find collected there on pages 190-192 some at any rate of the Roman-Dutch authorities on this point, and they seem to show that the Common law power of a Judge to deal with contempts is a somewhat restricted one. Those authorities lay stress on the fact that a Judge in punishing a contempt alleged to have been committed against his own authority, is very much in the position of a man sitting as judge in his own cause, and they would seem to limit his authority to cases where the contempt has been committed in the face of the Court or is otherwise so " notorious" that no practical doubt can arise as to the facts. Apparently, then, if the contempt alleged were not notorious but required " proof" for its establishment, the jurisdiction at Common law of the Judge against whose authority the contempt is alleged would be ousted, and it would be necessary to go to a higher tribunal if the contempt were to be investigated and punished. As far then as we can discover, the Common law rules as to this question make against the argument of the learned Councel for the respondent. This preliminary objection then, that the District Court of Ratnapura has jurisdiction in this matter and not the Supreme Court, cannot be sustained. We were also asked to say that the charge against the respondent as stated in the rule was not definite; it referred explicitly to the order of July 29 but did not specify the order of August 4. But the words " did withdraw a sum of Rs. 52,000 from the bank when permission had been granted to withdraw only a sum of Rs. 22,000" rehearse the substance of the order of August 4, and can refer to it and to nothing else; this was formally admitted by learned Counsel for the respondent. He was also 44 asked to say if any prejudice had been caused to the respondent by the wording of the rule, and he was unable to say that any had been caused to the respondent. The witnesses gave evidence as to the order of August 4 without any objection by learned Counsel for the respondent, who like everybody else concerned understood from the first that the order of August 4 and what happened with regard to it were just as much part of the charge as the order of July 29. The objection that the charge contained in the rule was not sufficiently definite, fails. The legal effect of the orders remains to be considered. By that of July 29 probate was to issue but the money in the bank was not to be drawn without permission of Court. On August 4 probate was granted to the respondent and simultaneously an order was issued restraining him from drawing more than Rs. 22,000 from the bank. It was urged that legally the Court could not grant probate to the executor and at the same moment restrict that probate; if the probate was good, as clearly it was, then the restriction was irregular. This may be conceded, but it was urged for the respondent that the order of the August 4 was not merely irregular but fundamentally bad as having been made without jurisdiction, and that it was a nullity which the respondent could disregard. If this order of August 4 was merely an order irregularly issued by a competent Court, then the principle would apply that the order must be obeyed by the party affected until the order be discharged (Silva v. Appuhamy [ 1 4 N. L. R. 178.]). See also Russell v. East Anglian Railway Co. [ 2 3 Mac. & G. 104, 42 E. R. 2OI, at p. 206.] where it is stated, " It is an established rule of this Court (the Court of Chancery) that it is not open to any party to question the orders of this Court, or any process issued under the authority of this Court, by disobedience. I know of no act which this Court may do which may not be questioned in a proper form, and on a proper application; but I am of opinion that it is not competent for anyone to interfere with the possession of a receiver or to disobey an injunction or any other order of the Court on the ground that such orders were improvidently made. Parties must take a proper course to question their validity, but while they exist they must be obeyed''. See also Fennings v. Humphrey [ 3 4 Beav. 1, 49, E. R. 237 at p. 239.], " It is clear that a party who is served with an order, may be guilty of contempt for disobedience, in a case in which the order ought not to have been made. He is not to determine for himself but ought to come to the Court for relief, if advised, if the order is invalid". These are well known principles which affect the order of a competent Court irregularly or improvidently made, but it was urged that the order of August 4 was not merely irregular or improvident, but one made without jurisdiction at all, and not the order of a competent Court; probate to the executor of the will is an absolute permission which cannot, in the absence of special circumstances not pretended to exist here, be fettered in any way. This argument omitted to notice the effect of Ordinance No. 8 of 1919, section 23 (4), saying that no probate shall issue from a District Court unless and until a certificate has been filed that estate duty has been paid or security for its payment given to the satisfaction of the Commissioner of Stamps. 45 But the argument must be considered in detail. A District Court has beyond question full testamentary jurisdiction-this was a testamentary case-by section 69 of the Courts Ordinance and by Chapters 38, 54, and 55 of the Civil Procedure Code. If the order impugned as made without jurisdiction had been worded, "subject to the respondent securing Rs. 30,000 for payment of estate duty in accordance with section 23 of Ordinance No. 8 of 1919 by leaving that amount in the bank, probate to issue", no objection of want of jurisdiction could have been taken. Such an order might-indeed would-have been in an unusual form, but without question there would have been jurisdiction to make it and, in making it, the Court would have been doing its plain duty under the statute. If the order had been worded, " Issue probate, but simultaneously the respondent must undertake to secure Rs. 30,000 for payment of estate duty in accordance with Ordinance No. 8 of 1919 by leaving that amount in the bank", it would again have been irregular in form, but again no question of want of jurisdiction could arise, for again the Court would have been doing the plain duty imposed upon it by statute. If the order last suggested, instead of being in one document, had been split up into two, could it then have been argued that there was want of jurisdiction? For, again the Court would have been doing its statutory duty. Now the only difference between an order just supposed i.e., the order of probate in one document, and the order restricting withdrawal from the bank in another-and that of August 4, is that the latter contained no reference to or mention of the Ordinance or estate duty. Yet the Rs. 30,000 were reserved to secure estate duty and for no other purpose. Evidence which was admitted without objection and which must be accepted says so, and the law (Ordinance No. 8 of 1919) requires this as condition precedent to the grant of probate. The Court then was in an imperfect and irregular manner doing the duty imposed on it by law, something which it not only had jurisdiction to do but which it was required to do by an imperative provision in a statute. This order then of August 4, that is the grant of probate and the accompanying restriction on withdrawal from the bank, was irregular and improvident and open to objection on several points, but it is impossible to say that it was issued without jurisdiction. It was then an order to be obeyed until set aside by competent process, and disobedience to it could have been restrained by injunction and can be punished as for a contempt. Further, the grant of probate and the order of August 4 contained an undertaking by the respondent. He got probate, and in getting it undertook to leave the balance Rs. 30,000 to the credit of the case. " The breach of an undertaking given to the Court by a person or corporation pending proceedings, on the faith of which the Court sanctions a particular course of action or inaction, is misconduct amounting to contempt"- 7 Halsbury, 2nd ed., p. 35, s. 51. See also Gallow v. Young [ 1 56L.T.147.], which was a case on the difference between commitment and attachment, a distinction not known to our law, but where the principle laid down in the passage from Halsbury just quoted is assumed without question. See also D. v. A. & Co.[ 2 (19OO) 1 C h 484], which reaffirms the principle that where a person 46 has voluntarily given an undertaking that undertaking can be enforced by committal. The case of Neath Canal Co. v. Ynisarwed Resolven Colliery Coy. [ 1 L. R. 10, Ch. A. 450.] is further authority to the same effect. It does not lie in anyone's mouth to obtain an advantage in return for an undertaking, and then to say, I will retain the advantage but will not be bound by the undertaking which has obtained me that advantage. The argument that this order of August 4, or the undertaking given thereon by the respondent, was a nullity, or made without jurisdiction, or by a Court other than a competent Court, cannot be sustained. On August 6, two days later that is, the respondent went to the Imperial Bank in Colombo, saw the Manager, Mr. Ian Stewart, introduced himself as the executor, stated that he had received probate, and says that he handed him the probate and a certified copy of the will, which he had had prepared the day before. Mr. Stewart, he says, handed the two documents to an officer of the bank and asked him to register the probate. The respondent then asked the Manager to transfer the money standing to the credit of the estate into his account as executor. He then says as follows : " Then I told Mr. Stewart I had permission of the Court to draw Rs. 22,000. I showed him the copy of the journal entry and he said, this is not necessary, it is de trop ". The respondent then signed a cheque for the full amount and it was transferred to his account as executor, and he left the bank. This was the account of the matter he gave to us. In a letter of June 30, 1933, to the District Judge, Ratnapura, who had asked him to explain why the balance had been withdrawn when the Court had said it must not be without order of Court, he had said, " When probate was issued to me, I took it to the Imperial Bank with the letter of Court giving me permission to withdraw a sum of Rs. 22,000. This letter of authority was not required but the letters of probate were registered and the full amount that was lying with them in the name of the deceased was transferred to a new account in my name as executor and I was informed that I was free to draw upon this account. I was not aware that the Court had placed any prohibition on my drawing any more than Rs. 22,000. Had I been aware of it I would not have disregarded the wishes of the Court and I wish to tender my apology to the Court." The phrase " not required" is studiously vague. It can mean, and taken with the context probably does, that he did not consider it was required, but if he really had showed it to a bank official, it is remarkable that he did not say so in that letter. To complete the story of his dealings with this account. He drew out some Rs. 14,000 in the month of August, 1931, and continued to draw on the account until by the end of the year it had fallen to Rs. 10,000, by February, 1932 to Rs. 4,917, and by the end of May, 1932. ten months after his having obtained probate, only Rs. 68.76 remained in the account. The Manager, Mr. Stewart, gave evidence before us to a very different effect. He says that the respondent did come to him but that he showed him the probate only. He had this registered and adopted the usual procedure, namely, opened a fresh account in the name of the executor. He adds, "The executor drew a cheque for the amount and a cheque book was issued to him to freely operate on the new account. At that 47 interview he gave me nothing except the probate, no copy of an order of Court". He said that he did not think that a copy of the will was shown to him, though he added that he would not deny that this might have been done, but he was perfectly clear that no order of Court was ever shown to him and that he never saw the order of August 4 saying that Rs. 30,000 must remain to the credit of the case. A copy of the bank register was put in. It mentions the probate but not the will or any order. The probate handed to the executor bears the bank stamp, but the copy of the will which he says he showed to Mr. Stewart has no bank stamp upon it, nor the copy of the journal entry which he says he showed to Mr. Stewart and which he produced to us in Court. In examination in chief Mr. Stewart said, " Had I been aware that the respondent was given permission only to draw up to Rs. 22,000 I would have allowed him to draw out what was allowed and would have registered that order with the probate ", and in cross-examination he said, " If the document like that shown to me was produced with the probate I would only have transferred Rs. 22,000 to the executor's new account. That document qualifies the probate. I would read the certified copy of the journal entry as a prohibitory order and I would at the same time consult my solicitors, . . . . When there is any prohibition we are very careful and in cases of the slightest doubt we consult our solicitors ", and to the Court he said, " I would have considered the journal entry to be a prohibition on me as well. A bank has to be very careful", and he stated that it was not likely he said the words attributed to him by the respondent. The whole matter seems to have slumbered until November 15, 1932, on which date there is a journal entry in this testamentary case No. 971, " The Commissioner of Stamps informs that assessment of estate duty as provisionally assessed by him is Rs. 26,545.51, with interest thereon from September 7, 1931, to date of payment, and notice of assessment has been issued on the executor. On payment of the duty the certificate under section 23 (3) of the Estate Duty Ordinance will be issued". Other journal entries of no particular importance occur, and then there is one on January 27, 1933, in which the proctors for the executor ask that the matter which had been fixed for February 22 should be postponed and called some day after March 17, 1933, as the executor was making arrangements with the Commissioner of Stamps to pay estate duty " which is only partly assessed now ". This order was refused, and eventually the executor, respondent to this rule, appeared in the District Court, Ratnapura, on March 2, and stated that he was in communication with the Commissioner of Stamps to pay the amount by instalments and that he would get the Commissioner of Stamps to communicate with the Court. The position then at the end of 1932 and early in 1933 was this. The Court officials at Ratnapura knew that probate had been given but did not know that anything more than the Rs. 22,000 allowed had been drawn out, until inquiry at the bank in February, 1933, informed them that the whole amount had been transferred to an account opened by the executor. The Commissioner of Stamps did not even know that probate had been given, so he could not know that anything had been withdrawn from the account. On December 12, 1932, and February 21, 1933, he wrote letters to the executor asking when he might expect payment of 48 the estate duty but received no reply to either. But as the executor had been told that he had to appear on March 17, 1933, in the Court at Ratnapura, he realized he must do something, so he obtained an interview with the then Commissioner of Stamps, Mr. Prasad, on March 4, 1933. At this interview he suggested reasons for the delay connected with the properties in the schedule and asked to be allowed till August before paying duty. Mr. Prasad's note of interview made the same day, contains the following : " I suggested to him that duty be paid out of the money in the bank. He said that is the only cash belonging to the estate and that arrangements are being made to pay the duty without touching the deposits in the bank". At that time to his knowledge the amount of money left in the bank was Rs. 13.76, having through his drawings fallen to that balance credit by February, 1933, the rest of the Rs. 52,000 having been drawn and spent by him. Consequently this that he said to Mr. Prasad-and before us he admitted the accuracy of Mr. Prasad's note-was a suggestio falsi and suppressio veri of a very serious character. What he said was meaningless unless there still was money in the bank from which to pay this estate duty. It was a deliberate deception of the person with whom he was dealing. It is now possible to examine the credibility of the executor, respondent to this rule. In Mr. Prasad and Mr. Stewart we have two perfectly independent witnesses. To Mr. Prasad he has said things deliberately deceptive. By Mr. Stewart he is contradicted on a matter where all the probabilities are that his version is false and that of Mr. Stewart true ; we accept the version of Mr. Stewart as to what happened between them on August 6, 1931, and disbelieve that of the respondent. If we apply these findings, and reason from them, as we must, then we are driven to the conclusion that the respondent to this rule has no hesitation in telling a false story when he thinks it necessary to further his interest. This confirms us in the opinion we had formed aliunde that his account of the events of August 4, 1931, must be rejected. The facts are then that the respondent knew on August 4, 1931, of the order of July 29 and its prohibition, that on August 6, 1931, he concealed from Mr. Stewart the journal entry of August 4 and did not show it to him. and that on March 4, 1933, he deceived Mr. Prasad as to the money which had been in the bank. Resuming the narrative of events. Immediately after the interview of March 4, 1933, Mr. Prasad got wind of the fact that probate had been issued and the money withdrawn. He inquired of the then District Judge, Ratnapura, why probate had been issued and the money allowed to be withdrawn, and all the District Judge could say was that it had been done by a predecessor of his. He wrote to the executor himself on March 9. saying that as the money had been withdrawn by him on August 6, 1931. he could not allow payment of the estate duty to be postponed till August, but required that it should be paid or secured by hypothecation within one month. The respondent replied next day asking for time till June and making no reference to having drawn out the money. Then on May 30, 1933, the District Judge, Ratnapura, wrote to the respondent asking him why the. balance was withdrawn when the Court had made order that it should not be withdrawn without the permission of the Court. 49 The respondent took a month to answer this letter and then on June 30, 1933, wrote the letter quoted from already in which he stated that he was not aware that the Court had placed any prohibition on his drawing more than Rs. 22,000, that had he been aware of it he would not have disregarded the orders of the Court and that he wished to tender an apology. He added, " I withdrew the money from time to time as under the will it belonged solely to me, and specially as a minute of consent from the other respondent had been filed in Court". His claim that the money was his seems to conflict with what he had told Mr. Prasad three months before, namely, that it was cash belonging to the estate. All that can be said of this letter of June 30 from the respondent is, that it shows that he had already at that date determined to tell the story that he has told to us. namely, that he did not regard the words in the order of August 4 as to leaving Rs. 30,000 to the credit of the case, as a prohibition on his drawing that Rs. 30,000. Again resuming the narrative of events. The respondent was told on July 12 to file accounts in the case within one month, also that the case would be called on August 14. He waited till August 11 before writing, and then asked that he might have till the end of September to file his accounts. He was given till October 2 within which to do so. There was yet further delay on the part of the respondent, but finally on November 2 he did file an intermediate account showing the sum of Rs. 16,677.73 as expenditure incurred. No vouchers were submitted though it is usual to do so with intermediate accounts. On November 17 the then Commissioner of Stamps, Mr. Huxham, wrote to the executor sending a final total assessment of Rs. 50,290.17 for estate duty, and informed the District Court, Ratnapura, of this on November 20. The respondent says that he never received this letter of November 17. On December 19, 1933, the Commissioner wrote again to the executor inviting answer to the letter of November 17, again on January 16, 1934, and again on February 9, 1934. The respondent did not answer any of these three letters. The respondent was served with a citation to appear in the Court at Ratnapura on April 3. Consequently, he could no longer ignore the matter and at last on March 27 he wrote to the Commissioner of Stamps admitting that he had been " somewhat lax " in his proceedings. In the letter he proposes to pay a sum of Rs. 10,000 to Rs. 15,000 if in the meantime certain lands may be released from seizure; the date of this seizure was not given us in evidence. But the gist of the letter was a request that the inquiry in the Ratnapura Court listed far April 3 should be put off. He seems to have had an interview with the Commissioner on the same date as his letter, March 27, at which it was agreed that there was a possibility that the letter from the Commissioner of Stamps of November 17 containing the final assessment had not reached him, but he did not deny having received the three letters following it, asking for an answer. Those letters referred to the possibly missing letter of November 17, and if he had had any anxiety to settle this debt he would have written at once to the Commissioner of Stamps saying that he had never received the letter of November 17, and asking for a copy. Other conferences with the Commissioner of Stamps seem to have followed, which are summarised in the latter's letter 50 of May 25, 1934, namely, that the respondent had proposed to pay within a month or so Rs. 10,000 in cash and that a certain 3,492 acres was to be transferred to Government in settlement of estate duty. The letter ends, " I will remind you that the question of payment comes before the District Court, Ratnapura, again on July 3 and that if the negotiations in question are not concluded by that date my officer will have no alternative than to request the Court to issue a writ to the Fiscal". It should be mentioned that the total sum due for estate duty had now been reduced from the Rs. 50,290.17 of the previous November and fixed at Rs. 34,108 with interest at 4 per cent from September 7, 1931. On June 9 the Commissioner of Stamps had to write to the respondent saying that he had had no answer to his letter of May 25, nor confirmation of the agreement as to the final assessment. At last on June 19 respond-ant did write to the Commissioner confirming the arrangement and asking permission to pay in five yearly instalments. On June 28 the acting Commissioner wrote to say that the Crown was prepared to take over the block of land referred to at the rate of Rs. 5 per acre, provided title was approved, pointing out that he had not yet received the Rs. 10,000 that the respondent had promised to pay. The respondent by letter of June 29 agreed to this, namely, to the Crown taking over the 3,492-acre block. He wrote again on July 2 explaining that he was unable to pay the Rs. 10,000 and laying the blame for this on the Government; his request apparently had been that the Crown should release its seizure of certain lands in return for his unsecured promise to pay Rs. 10,000. On September 5 the Commissioner wrote again to the executor saying that the Crown was prepared to take over in final settlement of the estate duties a certain block of 2,025 acres together with the 3,492 acres, provided that the lands in question were handed over free from encumbrances or outside interests of any kind and that the consent of all the beneficiaries and of the Court was given to such transfer. On September 10, 1934, the respondent consented to this, but, as always, asked for more time and more delay. On September 18, 1934, the respondent filed in the District Court a consent by himself and certain of the beneficiaries under the will in the case to transfer to the Crown the 3,492 acres and the 2,025 acres, but this document was imperfect since there were several beneficiaries, respondents in the testamentary case No. 971, whose consent had not been obtained. Here the matter rests for the moment. Of the Rs. 34,108 estate duty, some Rs. 2,547 has been paid, partly by rent due from the properties involved. The lands themselves are under seizure by the Crown but so far, except for the Rs. 2,547, the Crown has not received the estate duty payable on this estate. We have found as facts that the respondent had full knowledge of the order of July 29, 1931, and that he knew that the order of August 4, 1931, was a prohibition against his drawing from the bank more than Rs. 22,000, also that he had undertaken not to withdraw more than that amount but to leave undrawn the balance of Rs. 30,000 to meet estate duty. We are of opinion that it was legally the duty of the respondent to obey the order of August 4 and to conform to the undertaking he had given. We are satisfied that he wilfully disobeyed that order and wilfully broke the undertaking which he had given. We are of opinion that this was a very 51 serious contempt of Court in view of the entire and deliberate disregard of the law shown by the respondent, and in view of the large amount involved. Such a serious contempt requires punishment sufficient to mark the seriousness of the offence and to deter any other persons tempted in like case to offend and there is abundant authority that " the punishment should be commensurate with the offence; it may be severe where the contempt is grave ", Re Davies, 21 Q. B. D. at 238, as it certainly is here. We convict the respondent of this contempt and sentence him to simple imprisonment for a period of twelve months. DRIEBERG J.-I agree. AKBAR J.-I agree.
