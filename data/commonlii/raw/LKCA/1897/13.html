KONNEHAMY v. DE SILVA.

D. C, Matara, 779.

 

Evidence-Proof of payments on judgment-debt -Action for moneys over-paid-Civil Procedure- Code, s. 349-Costs.

Where a judgment-debtor sued, his creditor for recovery of moneys overpaid on the decree, held, that it was not competent to him to prove payments except as certified under section 349 of the Civil Procedure Code.

Held also that the costs in the Court below should be taxed as though the proceeding had been not an action, but a petition under section 349.

PLAINTIFF alleged that he was judgment-debtor in D. C. case No. 35,876; that his creditor (who was plaintiff in the latter case and defendant in the present case) recovered from him various sums of money, leaving a balance of only Rs. 486; that, notwithstanding, the defendant sued out a writ of execution for

66

Rs. 877 and recovered from him the whole of that amount. He now prayed that the defendant might be decreed to pay to plaintiff the sum of Rs. 391 recovered in excess from him by the defendant.

When the plaint was filed defendant took objection that the plaintiff ought to have proceeded by petition under section 349 of the Civil Procedure Code and had the alleged payments certified. The District Judge upheld the objection and returned the plaint to plaintiff, who thereupon filed a second plaint, which was open to a similar objection, but no objection having been taken the District Judge heard the case on the issues framed, viz.:-

(1)    Whether plaintiff had paid defendant two sums of Rs. 327 and Rs. 150 before the payment of Rs. 400 on October 31, 1892; and

(2)    Whether the two receipts which plaintiff produced, purporting to bear the signature of the defendants, for the two sums of money were forgeries or not.

The District Judge, after evidence heard, decided both the issues against the plaintiff. Plaintiff appealed.

Sampayo, for appellant. Dornhorst, for respondent.

8th October, 1896. Bonser, C.J.-

In his judgment the District Judge called attention to various matters which discredited the plaintiff's story, amongst them that the plaintiff was a trader and a man of business, and would not be likely to make such a mistake as to overpay his creditor; that he did not commence the action till six months after the discovery of his mistake: that the account which he gives of the payment of the first sum of Rs. 327 was contradicted by the evidence of Mr. Gooneratna, the Deputy Fiscal; that when he filed his plaint he did not mention the fact that he held these two receipts; that two of the witnesses whom he called to prove these payments were not mentioned in his original list of witnesses; that, the notarial receipt, which was admittedly given by the defendant on the last occasion when money was paid by the plaintiff to the defendant, contains no reference to this previous payment.

All these things influenced the mind of the District Judge and brought him to the conclusion that the story of the plaintiff was untrue. The evidence as to the handwriting is not of a very satisfactory character, and the District Judge was justified in attaching little weight to it.

67

Nothing which has been said by Mr. Sampayo has satisfied me that the District Judge has arrived at a wrong conclusion. I am therefore of opinion that the appeal must be dismissed.

There is to my mind a fatal objection that the evidence as to these payments ought not to have been admitted contrary to the provisions of section 349 of the Civil Procedure Code. But we do not dismiss the appeal on that ground as we might, because the parties had evidently agreed to try these issues, and we consented to hear the appeal as though it had been an appeal from a petition by a judgment-debtor under section 349, which the Court had decided against the petitioner. But at the same time the costs in the Court below must be taxed as though the proceeding had been not an action, but a petition under section 349.

The respondent will have his costs of the appeal.

Withers, J.-

I am quite prepared to agree with the decision of the District Judge, and with the grounds he has assigned for his decision.

In the Matter of the Rule issued on MR. Edwin Beven, a Proctor of the Supreme Court,
practising at Kandy, to show cause why he should not
be suspended for malpractice.

 

Proctor and client-Malpractice-Issue by proctor of blank printed forms of letters of demand signed by him.

A proctor's signature to a letter of demand should be a pledge that the proctor has been satisfied, by examination and inquiry, that the claim to be enforced is at least an honest one; and to sign and issue in blank printed forms of letters of demand to be filled up by the client is most unprofessional conduct on the part of a proctor.

ON the complaint of the Chairman of the Dikoya Planters' Association, supported by an affidavit of Mr. Bonner, of Kirkoswald estate, charging Mr. Edwin Beven, Proctor, with " malpractice," the Supreme Court issued the following rule on Mr. Beven:-

Upon reading the affidavit of Arthur Calthrop Bonner, of Kirkoswald estate in Bogawantalawa, dated the 5th day of November. 1897, the letters of demand marked A and B, dated Kandy, the 30th June, 1897, and purporting to be made and issued by Edwin Beven, Proctor, on Muttusamy Kangany and on Sinna

68

Caruppen Kangany, respectively, for the immediate payment of the sum of Rs. 23.48 and Rs. 37.29, and the letter of the said Edwin Beven addressed to the said Arthur Calthrop Bonner, dated the 3rd July, 1897, in which the said Edwin Beven makes the following statement;-"I have no recollection of sending the letter you refer "to " (meaning letters A and B above referred to), and in which he further states: " Some of my clients have books containing printed " forms of letters of demand; they take my signature to these and " fill them as occasion occurs. It may be that the letters of demand " in question were so issued ": -It is ordered that the said Edwin Beven, a Proctor of the Supreme Court of the Island of Ceylon, do show cause before our said Court at Hulftsdorp on Thursday next, the 25th day of November, 1897, at 11 o'clock in the forenoon, why he should not be suspended from the said office of proctor, on the charge that he has been guilty of malpractice in supplying his clients with printed books containing blank letters of demand with his signature adhibited, and permitting them to fill in the said letters the names of their debtors and the amount of the claim against them, and so to use them or suffer others to use them as if they actually emanated from the said Edwin Beven.

On the returnable day of the rule Dornhorst (Wendt with him) submitted the following affidavit: -

" I, Edwin Beven, of Kandy, make oath and say as follows: -

"1. I am a Proctor of this Hon. Court, and as such have practised in Kandy for the last thirty years.

"2. I admit that in the case of certain of my standing clients, who are tradesmen doing a large business, with frequent occasion to issue letters of demand to their debtors, I have for their convenience and at their request from time to time signed a number of letters of demand contained in a bound book of printed forms, with foil and counterfoil, to be filled up by them as necessity arises. The letters so signed by me were intended for the exclusive use of my said clients to whom they were given, and I have never known of an instance until now in which any such client has passed on such letters to a stranger or has suffered others to use them.

"3. Although the rule served upon me and the affidavit upon which it was founded do not expressly charge me with selling ready-signed letters of demand, the following statement was made by the Chairman of the Maskeliya Planters' Association at a meeting held on the 2nd November instant, as reported in the Observer newspaper of the 5th November instant, which is hereto annexed and marked X: -' Only lately it has been discovered

69

' that blank books of signed proctor' letters of demand are sold to 'kaddy men, enabling them to put unfair pressure upon kanganies, ' thus unsettling our labour force.'

" I have reason to believe that the said Chairman referred to the letters of demand mentioned in the rule, and if that statement was meant to imply that these letters were sold by me, I say that the statement was unwarranted and not true. The letters were not sold by me, but were signed and given in the manner and for the reasons and purpose set out in the second paragraph hereof to M. Pitchey & Co., of Hatton, my clients, who are carrying on a large and extensive business as rice merchants. If the said Chairman or Mr. Bonner named in the said rule had the courtesy to write to me for an explanation, this reckless statement, so dangerous to the reputation of a professional man, would not have been publicly made.

"4. I had and have reason to believe that the practice is not uncommon of proctors leaving signed letters of demand in the hands of standing clients. The reason for this practice may be found in the nature of the business carried on by these clients, which necessitates the frequent and sometimes (as in the event of information being received by a tradesman in a planting district at a great distance from the residence of his proctor that a customer is about to suddenly quit the district or the Island without paying his debts) the prompt issue and service of a formal proctor's letter of demand.

"5. In adopting and following this practice, founded upon considerations of mutual convenience of proctor and client, I have never felt that it was open to objection. On or about the 3rd day of July last I received a note, hereto annexed and marked Y, from the said Mr. A. C. Bonner, sending two letters of demand signed by me, but not bearing the Kandy postmark. I replied in the letter quoted in the said rule, which he has thought fit to lay before this Hon. Court without notice to me or opportunity afforded me of explaining the circumstances under which and the reasons for which such signed letters are left in the hands of standing clients.

" 6. As stated in paragraph I hereof, I have been a proctor of this Hon. Court for a period of thirty years and have had an extensive practice, having especially numerous clients engaged in trade in the planting districts, and my conduct as such proctor has never previously been called in question. I have always made it my endeavour to maintain the dignity and integrity of the honourable profession to which I belong, and it has caused me considerable pain that, after so many arduous years of labour

70

and toil, the professional reputation which I have successfully maintained should have been publicly assailed before this Hon. Court, of which I am an officer, by a charge of malpractice.

"7. I further say that had 1 had the least suspicion that this practice was not strictly and professionally correct I should not have adopted it. This Hon. Court, however, by the language of the said rule, has characterized this practice as a malpractice.

" With all due respect and submission I have offered my explanation and justification of it, in the hope that, even if this Hon. Court does not accept such justification, it will accept my explanation as satisfactory and as exonerating me from the reproach attaching to conduct calling for the punishment of suspension."

Their Lordships, after hearing the explanation of counsel, expressed their dissatisfaction with it and the affidavit tendered, as Mr. Beven did not seem to realize the gravity of the offence. They were surprised that he did not offer any apology for his conduct or give them any assurance that the malpractice would not be continued, or that he had taken steps to recall the blank letters issued to his clients.

Mr, Dornhorst thereupon moved for a postponement in order to submit a supplementary affidavit. The application was allowed, and Mr. Dornhorst tendered to the Court the following affidavit: -

" I, Edwin Beven, of Kandy, make oath and say as follows: -

"1. I regret that the affidavit which was sworn to by me and read on my behalf in showing cause to the rule served upon me was insufficient, in that it did not contain any expression of my regret at having followed the practice condemned by the said rule. The omission was in no way due to any intention on my part to justify the said practice in opposition to the view of this Hon. Court. In my said affidavit I endeavoured to put before the Court the circumstances under which I came to adopt such a practice in extenuation of the blame which 1 had incurred.

"2. I now see the impropriety of the practice and express regret that I should have adopted it without duly considering the objections to which it was open and the abuse to which it was liable. I have since the service of the rule discontinued the practice and have not issued any blank letters of demand.

"3. To the best of my knowledge (I am making this affidavit in Colombo) these printed books are in the hands of not more than six of my clients, and I shall at once call in all signed blank letters of demand outstanding in the hands of my clients, and I do hereby undertake that such letters shall never be issued by me again."

Cur. adv. vult.

71

26th November, 1897. Lawrie, A.C.J.-

Mr. Beven, in the affidavit presented and read to us yesterday, states that he, for the convenience and at the request of traders doing a large business, has from time to time signed a number of letters of demand to be filled up by them as necessity arises. He stated that he has reasons to believe that the practice is not uncommon of proctors leaving letters of demand in the hands of standing clients. He stated that the reason for the practice may be found in the nature of the business carried on by these clients, which necessitates the frequent and sometimes-(for instance, as in the event of information being received by a tradesman in a planting district at a great distance from the residence of his proctor that a customer was about to suddenly quit the district in the Island without paying his debt)-the prompt issue of letters of demand, Mr. Beven stated that he had not the least suspicion that this practice was not strictly professionally correct. It is therefore necessary for the Supreme Court to express its ruling very clearly. It is the opinion of this Court that all writings which bear the signature of a proctor must be read and approved of before they are signed and issued. Letters of demand make an important-stage in the relation of debtor and creditor. They are a step of legal procedure, for the cost of which a proctor is entitled to recover, not only from his client, but from the losing party, if a litigation ensues. Letters of demand contain direct statements that the proctor has received instructions to demand from the person addressed immediate payment of a sum of money; that he has been further instructed to sue the person at law for the recovery of the amount and costs, and a letter of demand concludes with an expression of the proctor's personal opinion. Now, it is surely plain that if a proctor signs a blank letter, with the intention that all the essential parts of it shall be filled up, he allows his signature to appear as vouching for as many false statements as there are sentences in the letter. No proctor should sign a letter of demand even fully filled up unless he has reason to believe that the demand he then makes is a just demand. This Court is entitled to demand of every proctor that his signature cannot be used for the purpose of making a false, unjust, or oppressive demand. No proctor can be permitted to give to clients, who, however respectable, are necessarily interested, license to use his name to writings which imply that he has been consulted by his client, and that he writes as a professional man on instructions and as giving professional advice. The intimation that a gentleman of Mr. Beven's standing had originally no

72

suspicions that the practice of issuing blank letters of demand was wrong, makes it necessary to check the practice by a sentence of suspension, which I pronounce with great regret, for I have known Mr. Beven intimately for the last twenty-four years, and I well know and give willing testimony to his great worth and ability. I am glad to have road his affidavit of to-day. It is with reluctance and pain that I still feel obliged to propose to my colleagues that the sentence be one of suspension from the office of proctor for three months.

Withers, J.-

Mr. Beven's original answer to the charge of malpractice in furnishing his clients with blank forms of letters of demand which bear his signature, that he saw no wrong in it, I confess amazed me. It is a relief to see by his supplementary affidavit that his eyes have been opened in this regard. To write to-day of a possible claim hereafter that one has been instructed to make a particular demand is simply a false representation. A proctor's signature to a letter of demand should be a pledge that the proctor has been satisfied by examination and inquiry that the claim to be enforced is at least an honest one. To sign and issue letters of the kind is surely most unprofessional conduct. Then, look at the mischief of the thing as well. There is the obvious danger in issuing such letters that they may find their way into the hands of unscrupulous persons, who will use them for purposes of extortion. It is painful to have to bring home to a proctor of Mr. Beven's standing a sense of the impropriety of a course of conduct in his profession, but I think we should be failing in our duty if we did not mark our sense of his misconduct.

Browne, A.J.-

In a few words I would express my concurrence with the opinions of my lord and my brother, and share in their regret that it has been found necessary to pass this sentence of suspension on a professional gentleman of such long standing. It is however impossible not to say that the practice which they thereby discountenance is, in short, an improper one. We have known before now in the District Court the importance not only of the proper issuing of letters of demand, but also of the service of such letters, especially when a question of costs was involved, but I take a wider view than this. I think the practice reprehensible, because the Court issued authority and duty to professional gentlemen to safeguard the interests of litigants in their courts

73

to the extent that they should not come in unless they had bona fide and proper cause of action, and it is not right that duty or authority should be delegated to any one. The practice is reprehensible, too, as it seems open to the construction that it is an attempt to induce business by that means, which is not proper conduct for a professional man, while my learned brother has pointed out the extreme danger of allowing these letters going out. I see one element in the character of the persons to whom I understand these books were addressed. They might be utilized as a menace towards people of such little knowledge as Sinhalese villagers or Tamil coolies, who might be frightened by such a letter being handed them almost as much as by a process of the Court. I concur in the view that the practice is reprehensible, and think it is necessary to pass this sentence.
