Provincial Councils Elections Act, No. 2 of 1988-S.58. S.92(1)(a) and (b), S.97 -North Western Provincial Council - Election of Members - Election Petition - S.91(a) Presidential Elections Act - Compared - Contrasted - Intimidation - Non Compliance with provisions of the Act - Other circum-stances.

The Petitioners relied on the following grounds for the avoidance of the Election of members of the North Western Provincial Council - Administrative District of Puttalam:

(a) General intimidation;

(b) Non compliance with the provisions of the Provincial Councils Act;

(c) Other circumstances within S.92(1)(a) of the Provincial Councils Election Act

The Respondents raised the following preliminary objections

(1) (i) The Petitioners have failed to identify which recognized political party or independent group would have secured the votes of that section of the electors who was prevented from voting but for such general intimidation/or such acts of non-compliance.

66

(ii) The Petitioners have failed to aver and plead as a material fact - the specific number of votes which recognized political party or the independent group would have secured but for such intimidation/or such acts of non-compliance in order to establish that the result was in fact affected.

(2) (i) The Petitioners have failed to identify which recognized political party or independent group would have secured the votes of that section of the electors which they claim was prevented from voting but for such acts of non - compliance.

(ii) The Petitioners have failed to aver and plead as a material fact the specific number of votes which recognized political party or independent group would have secured but for -such acts of non compliance" in order to establish that the result was in fact affected.

(3) The facts pleaded do not constitute 'other circumstances' within the meaning of S. 92(1)(a) and accordingly do not constitute a ground in law.

(4) The Petitioners have failed to aver and plead the material facts required to establish that by reason of other circumstances' the result was materially affected.

(5) The Petitioners have failed to name all the candidates who contested as Respondents.

Held

(i) In terms of S.92(a) and (b) the Petitioners have to establish not only the occurrence of general intimidation or other circumstances, but also show that on account of such general intimidation the result was materially affected.

The Petitioners must specify which recognized Political party or Independent Group would have received the largest number of votes and seats in place of the political party whose name appears in the result declared under S.58.

(ii) It is incumbent on the Petitioners to establish that the result was materially affected. The Petitioners must expressly aver that acts constituting general intimidation relied upon by them operated either to confer a benefit on the Respondents or adversely affected the Petitioners.

67

(iii) The Petitioners have not set out the consequences of such noncompliance as being that some of the voters who supported the Petitioner were unable to vote. The Petitioners have failed to set out the consequences which flowed from such non-compliance and further has failed to aver that the said acts of non-compliance -did operate to materially affect them adversely.

(iv) It is necessary to aver and plead the identity of the Party or group which was affected by such other circumstances.

"Such other circumstances" are wide enough to include instances of non-compliance with the law relating to the conduct of elections.

(v) The Petitioners do not claim a declaration that they have been duly elected. Therefore the requirement is to name all the returned candidates and all persons against whom allegations of corrupt practices have been made with which requirement the Petitioners have complied.

WEERASURIYA, J.

The petitioners have relied on three grounds set out below for the avoidance of the election of members of the North Western Provincial Council in respect of the Administrative District of Puttalam.

(1) General intimidation

(2) Non-compliance with the provisions of the Provincial Councils Act; and

(3) Other circumstances within the meaning of section 92(1) (a) of the Provincial Councils Elections Act

3rd to 11th, 19th, 29th to 35th respondents (E.P. No. 02/99) raised the following preliminary objections in respect of both petitions.

(1) The petitioners have failed to aver and plead therein material facts required by law to be established that by reason of the acts of general intimidation pleaded in the petitions that the result of the election was materially affected in that

(a) the petitioners have failed to identify which recognized political party or independent group would have secured the votes of that section of the electors who was prevented from voting but for such general intimidation;

(b) that, the petitioners have failed to aver and plead as a material fact, the specific number of votes which recognized political party or the independent group would have secured but for such intimidation in order to establish that the result was in fact affected.

(2) The petitioners have failed to aver and plead the material facts, required by law to be established that by reason of the

69

acts of non-compliance, the result of the election was materially affected in that

(a) the petitioners have failed to identify which recognized party or independent group would have secured the votes of that section of the electors which they claim was prevented from voting but for such acts of non-compliance;

(b) the petitioners have failed to aver and plead as a material fact, the specific number of votes which recognized political party or independent group would have secured but for such acts of non-compliance in order to establish that the result was in fact affected.

(3) The facts pleaded by the petitioners do not constitute 'other circumstances' within the meaning of Section 92(1)(a) of Act, No. 2 of 1988 and accordingly do not constitute a ground in law.

(4) In any event, the petitioners have failed to aver and plead the material facts, required by law to establish that by reason of `other circumstances' the result of the election was materially affected in that:

(a) the petitioners have failed to identify which recognized political party or independent group would have secured the votes of that section of the electors which they claim was prevented from voting but for such 'other circumstances',

(b) the petitioners have failed to aver and plead as a material fact the specific number of votes which recognized political party or independent group would have secured but for the occurrence of such 'other circumstances' in order to establish that the result was in fact affected.

70

(5) The petitioners have failed to name all the candidates who contested the election as respondents.

Learned President's Counsel for the petitioners in both petitions contended that

(1) the law does not require that such matters be pleaded in the petition;

(2) the petitioners have pleaded the material facts that are required to be pleaded in terms of Section 98 of the Provincial Councils Elections Act;

(3) whether or not the result of the election was materially affected is a matter of proof which the Court will have to decide at the end of the case;

(4) since the petitioners do not claim a declaration that the petitioners or any other candidate have been duly elected, it is not necessary to name as respondents all the contesting candidates in terms of Section 97 of the Provincial Councils Elections Act.

Section 92(1) (a) and (b) of the Provincial Councils Elections Act No. 2 of 1988 read as follows

92(1). The election in respect of any administrative district shall be declared to be void on an election petition on the following grounds which may be proved to the satisfaction of the Election Judge namely

(a) that by reason of general bribery, general treating or general intimidation or other misconduct or other circumstances whether similar to those enumerated before or not a section of electors was prevented from voting for the recognized political party or independent group which it preferred and thereby materially affected the result of the election;

71

(b) non-compliance with the provisions of this Act relating to elections, if it appears that the election was not conducted in accordance with the principles laid down in such provisions and that such noncompliance materially affected the result of the election.

It is manifest therefore, that a unique feature of this provision is the requirement that apart from the occurrence of the requisite facts, which constitute the aforesaid grounds, there should be in addition proof of a certain consequence, namely that the result was materially affected.

On a plain reading of Section 92(1) (a) of the Provincial Councils Elections Act, it is clear that the charge of general intimidation set out therein postulates the following ingredients

(1) That intimidation is of a widespread character;

(2) That by reason of such general intimidation a certain result or consequence followed namely that a section of the electors was prevented from voting for the recognized political party or independent group which it preferred; and

(3) Thereby materially affected the result of the election.

Section 58 of the Provincial Councils Elections Act sets out what constitutes the result namely

(a) the number of votes received by each recognized political party and independent group after aggregating the ballot papers counted in each administrative district together with the postal votes; and.

(b) the number of preferences indicated for each candidate nominated by each party or group.

72

The Supreme Court in Bandaranaike Vs. Premadasa(1) at 261 in its preliminary order accepted the position that it is necessary for the petitioner to prove that several instances or acts of general intimidation had the result or consequence that the majority of electors were or may have been prevented from electing the candidate whom they preferred. However, Court held that on a fair reading of the relevant averments in the petition, the test formulated in Wijewardena Vs. Senanayake(2) was satisfied.

Commenting on this requirement, the Supreme Court in the final judgment in Bandaranaike Vs. Premadasa(3) at 13 stated as follows

"This passage shows that the view .of the court was that proof of widespread violence directed towards preventing electors from voting was not enough. There was the requirement of proof of an additional and distinct ingredient of the charge. Relief which the petitioner has asked for under section 91 (a) of the Act will be granted subject to a finding by the Supreme Court that the general intimidation had the requisite effect".

It is noteworthy that in the Provincial Councils Elections Act there is a significant change in regard to the effect of the acts of general intimidation on the voters; the requirement being in terms of section 92(1)(a) that a section of electors was prevented from voting for the recognized political party or independent group which it preferred. On the other hand, Section 91 (a) of the Presidential Elections Act provides that majority of electors may have been prevented from electing the candidate they preferred. In the circumstances, it is to be observed that the expression may have been prevented' connotes a mere probability or a likelihood whereas the expression `was prevented' lays down a certainty or a near certainty.

The words `materially affected' in section 92(a) and (b) of the Provincial Councils Elections Act would mean that where

73

there are several recognized political parties and independent groups, success of one political party or group over the other. The petitioners have to establish not only the occurrence of general intimidation or other circumstances, but also show that on account of such general intimidation the result was materially affected. The petitioners therefore, must specify which recognized political party or independent group would have received the largest number of votes and seats in place of the political party whose name appears in the result declared in terms of section 58 of the Provincial Councils Act.

The petitioner in election petition bearing No, 04/99, in paragraph 9(A) has stated that by reason of occurrence of the incidents, therein mentioned and the commission of acts specified therein, there was general intimidation of the electors at the aforesaid election in consequence of which a section of electors was prevented from voting for the recognized political party or the independent group which it preferred and thereby materially affected the result of the said election. It is noteworthy that the petitioner has failed to assert that he claims to have had a right to be returned or elected at the said election.

The petitioner in election petition bearing No. 02/99 while claiming to have had a right to be returned or elected at the said election, in paragraph 9(a) has set out that by reason of the occurrence of the incidents and the commission of the acts therein mentioned, there was general intimidation of a section of the electors of the administrative district of Puttalam and 'they were or may have been prevented' from voting for the recognized political party or independent group which they preferred and thereby materially affected the result of the election. It would be seen that petitioners in both petitions have mechanically reiterated the legal requirements for the grounds of avoidance in terms of Section 91 (a) of the Provincial Councils Elections Act. Since this is an essential constituent element in establishing the grounds of avoidance on general intimidation, there must be a corresponding averment setting

74

out the material facts that establish the alleged material change in the declared result. Thus, the petitioners in both election petitions have failed to aver indicating the identity of the recognized political party or independent group affected by the section of voters who were prevented from voting.

Learned President's Counsel for the 3rd to 11th,19th,29th to 35 respondents (E.P. No. 02/99) submitted further that the petitioners have failed to aver and plead as a material fact the specific number of votes which recognized political party or independent group would have secured but for such intimidation in order to establish that the result was in fact materially affected. This may entail mathematical proof of the number of voters prevented from voting for the political party of their choice to bring about a material alteration of the result.

In Bandaranaike Vs. Premadasa(1)(supra) at 269 it was observed that, the burden to prove that the majority of electors were in fact prevented is difficult and it is almost impossible to produce the requisite proof. It was observed that the word `may' in the Presidential Elections Act was designedly used because mathematical proof that the majority of electors were in fact prevented is impossible of attainment. It would be relevant to note that the Supreme Court was at pains to highlight the difference between the words 'were' and 'may have been'.

It is to be observed that under the Presidential Elections Act, the word 'may' would relieve a petitioner of a heavy burden. This soft option is not available to a petitioner under the Provincial Councils Elections Act because of words `was prevented from electing the candidate whom they preferred'. Therefore, it is incumbent on the petitioner to establish that the result was materially affected. The petitioners must expressly aver that acts constituting general intimidation relied upon by them operated either to confer a benefit on the respondents or adversely affected the petitioners.

75

Learned President's Counsel for the petitioner in election petition No. 02/99 submitted that, in view of the widespread nature of the acts constituting general intimidation, the petitioner would be hard put to identify exactly which electors were affected by any particular incident. He contended that this proposition is an impossibility which precludes a petitioner to maintain a petition on the ground of general intimidation. and that the law does not require one to do that which is impossible and impracticable. Furthermore, it was contended that the secrecy of the ballot precludes the petitioner from ascertaining how any particular elector would have voted.

In the case of Ratnam Vs. M. Dingiri Bandar and Pelpola Vs. R.S.S. Gunawardena(5) the Supreme Court acted upon the evidence of group leaders of voters and set aside the election on the ground of general intimidation. In both these cases, Court specifically addressed its mind to the pattern of voter behavior. Thus, evidence of the party affiliation of the voters was considered and acted upon despite secrecy provisions. Therefore, it would be seen that such an exercise is neither totally impossible nor impracticable.

In paragraph 7 of the petition bearing No. 02/99 and paragraph 4 of petition bearing No. 04/99, petitioners have averred that Peoples Alliance obtained 160,722 votes and the United National Party obtained 105,876 votes and Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna obtained 10,759 votes. Nevertheless, there is no averment indicating the number of votes U.N.P. and J.V.P. lost in consequence of alleged acts of general intimidation which exceeded the majority of 54,806.

The preliminary objection relating to the ground of avoidance by reason of the acts of non-compliance pleaded in paragraph 10(b) read with paragraph 12 of the petition bearing No. 02/99 was based on the same grounds namely

(a) the failure of the petitioner to identify which recognized political party or independent group

76

named by him in paragraph 7 would have secured the votes of that section of the electors who was prevented from voting;

(b) the failure of the petitioner to plead and aver as a material fact the specific number of votes which recognized political party or independent group would have secured but for such acts of non-compliance to establish that the result was materially affected.

Similarly, the same preliminary objection has been taken in respect of the ground of avoidance pleaded in paragraph 10(c) read with paragraph 13 of the petition bearing No. 02/99 to establish that by reason of `other circumstances' the result of .the election was materially affected.

Section 92(1)(b) of the Provincial Councils Elections Act postulates the following ingredients

(1) Non-compliance with provisions of the Act.

(2) Failure to conduct the election in accordance with the principles laid down in such provisions.

(3) Such non-compliance materially affected the result of the election

The petitioner in election petition bearing No. 02/99 has set out that armed groups having entered 71 polling booths where the total number of registered votes were approximately 110,000 which constitute 20% of the total number of polling stations and registered voters of the Puttalam District intimidated the polling staff and stuffed ballot papers into the ballot boxes. He has further stated that by reason of the aforesaid acts there was no free and fair election.

It would be clear that the petitioner has not proceeded to expressly set out the consequences of such non-compliance as being that some of the voters who supported the petitioner

77

were unable to vote. The petitioner has failed to set out in the petition the consequences which flowed from such noncompliance. Therefore, on a consideration of the totality of the averments in the petition, the petitioner has failed to aver that the said acts of non-compliance did operate to materially affect him adversely.

The material relating to the avoidance of the election on the ground of other circumstances being the same as spelt out in Section 92(1)(a), the ingredients referred to earlier would apply. Therefore, it is necessary to aver and plead the identity of the party or group which was affected by such other circumstances.

The contention of learned President's Counsel for the petitioners that whether or not the result of the election was materially affected is a matter of proof which the Court has to decide at the end of the case is untenable for the following reasons

(1) That section 98(c) of the Provincial Councils Elections Act stipulates that the petition must contain a concise statement of material facts on which the petitioner relies.

(2) In Wijewardena Vs. Senanayake (supra) it was observed that this requirement was intended to secure that a respondent will know from the petition itself what facts petitioner proposes to prove in order to avoid the election and will thus have a proper opportunity to prepare for the trial.

(3) The petitioners have failed to set out in the petitions, facts which are material and are necessary relating to the ingredients of the charges for the proof of their case.

The preliminary objection that the facts pleaded by the petitioners do not constitute `other circumstances' within

78

the meaning of section 92(1)(a) of the Provincial Councils Elections Act, has no merit as it was held in Bandaranaike Vs. Premadasa(1) (supra) that the words 'other circumstances' are wide enough to include instances of non-compliance with the law relating to the conduct of elections. The petitioners are entitled to plead instances of non-compliance to sustain a charge under other circumstances in terms of section 92(l) (a) of the Provincial Councils Elections Act.

The objection relating to the non-joinder of all the candidates who contested the election has to be rejected in view of the provisions of section 97 of the Provincial Councils Elections Act. It is to be observed that both petitioners do not claim a declaration that they have been duly elected. Therefore, the requirement is to name all the returned candidates and all persons against whom allegations of corrupt practices have been made with which the petitioners have complied.

For the foregoing reasons, I uphold the preliminary objections l, 2 and 4 referred to above, and dismiss both petitions with costs.

Preliminary Objection 1,2 and 4 upheld.

Application dismissed.
