THE QUEEN v. D. F. T. ABEYSINGHE and another

Trial at Bar No. 1 of 1965

Trial at Bar-Conditional pardon-Right of Attorney-General to withdraw it-Justiciability-Forum-Trial by Jury-Repeal ability of law providing for it- Partial invalidity of a statute-Doctrine of severability-Retrospective legislation- Validity-Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act No. 1 of 1062, ss. 6 (2) (a), 11 (1)-Criminal Law Act No. 31 of 1962, s. 3-Criminal Procedure Code, ss. 165 (/) (i), 216, 283 to 286, 391, 440A-Ceylon (Office of Governor-General) Letters Patent, 1947, s. 10-Penal Code, s. 115-Courts Ordinance, s. 19-Ceylon (Constitution) Order in Council, ss. 29, 39.

The two defendants had been granted a conditional pardon by the Attorney-General in terms of section 11 of the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act, No. 1 of 1962, which section was more or less an adaptation of section 283 of the Criminal Procedure Code with the necessary modifications to suit a Trial at Bar without a preliminary Magisterial inquiry. They were subsequently prosecuted at the present Trial at Bar, without a jury, for the offences in respect of which the pardon had been granted. The prosecution was instituted under

387

the provisions of the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act, No. 1 of 1962 and the Criminal Law Act, No. 31 of 1962.

An objection was taken in limine, on behalf of the defendants, that, having been given a conditional pardon upon the condition of making a full and true disclosure of the whole of the circumstances relative to the offences, the defendants were not liable to be prosecuted without proof of prior conviction in the District Court for having given false evidence.

Held, (i) that, while the Attorney-General has the power to withdraw a pardon tendered by him, the question whether there has been a breach of the condition of the pardon or not, whenever it is raised by the defence, is one which, by its very nature, is justiciable by a court.

(ii) that the trial court is the proper forum to decide the issue in regard to the violation of the conditions of the pardon.

(iii) that the right to trial by jury is not an entrenched provision of the law. It is within the power of Parliament, under section 29 of the Ceylon (Constitution) Order in Council, to repeal or amend the statutory provision of section 216 of the Criminal Procedure Code relating to trial by jury.

(iv) that where a statute is partly ultra vires and partly intra vites, the whole statute is not bad in law. In such a case, according to the doctrine of severability, the valid provisions are operative.

(v) that section 6 (2) (a) of the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act, No. 1 of 1962, amending section 115 of the Penal Code, is not invalid merely because of its retrospective character. Parliament has power to pass retroactive or retrospective legislation, provided it does not disregard a prohibition or limitation placed on its legislative power.

ORDER made in respect of a preliminary objection to a Trial at Bar which was held under the provisions of the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act No. 1 of 1962 and the Criminal Law Act No. 31 of 1962.

June 21, 1965. ORDER OF COURT-

On the 27th of February, 1965, the Attorney-General exhibited the Information in the terms set out in document "A" [1 See page 403 (infra).] against the two defendants, Don Frederick Terrence Abeysinghe and James Hugh Vernon de Alwis. On the 7th of May, 1965, this court made order requiring the defendants to appear before it on the 24th of May, 1965, and to answer in writing to the Information. The two defendants appeared on the 24th of May, 1965, in obedience to this summons, and tendered in writing what their counsel termed a plea-in-bar based on three grounds :-

(i) That the Information has been laid without warrant or authority and they are not liable to be tried thereon,

388

(ii) That the Information filed before the Court is bad in law and does not comply with the minimum requirements of an Information as known to the law.

(iii) That, having been given a conditional pardon upon the condition of making a full and true disclosure of the whole of the circumstances within their knowledge relative to that offence, they are not liable to be prosecuted without proof that the condition of the said pardon has been violated or defeated by reason of any act or omission for which they are solely responsible.

On the application of counsel for the defendants, the argument of this preliminary question was fixed for hearing on the 1st of June, 1965. In view of the nature of the plea, we considered it inappropriate to make an order in regard to bail at that stage for the reason that, if the defendants succeeded in their plea, it would have been an unwarranted assumption of jurisdiction on our part to make an order for remand or bail of persons who were not liable to be tried.

When the hearing commenced, Mr. Chitty preferred to argue the third ground first. In his submission, if he succeeded on this question, there would be a determination of the preliminary issue without any further argument being necessary in support of the first two grounds.

The two defendants are admittedly two witnesses who gave evidence in the earlier case of Trial at Bar. They had been given a conditional pardon by the Attorney-General on the condition of their making a full and true disclosure of the whole of the circumstances within their knowledge relative to the offences with which 24 defendants were charged, these offences being identically the same as those appearing in the Information exhibited against them.

The submission of counsel for the defendants is that, according to section 11 (1) of the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act, No. 1 of 1962 and section 285 of the Criminal Procedure Code, before the two defendants who had been granted a conditional pardon become liable to be tried, it has to be established to the satisfaction of a court that they either concealed something essential regarding the circumstances relating to the offences which have been laid against them or that they gave false evidence. In his submission, the conditional pardon tendered by the Attorney-General, when accepted by any person, assumes the form of a contract between the two parties and it will militate against a fundamental principle of justice if the decision whether there has been a breach of the contract is left in the hands of one of the contracting parties. He urged that, if the legislature intended that this question should be decided by the Attorney-General, there would have been express provision to that effect. He, therefore, argues that it is a court that must decide whether a person, who has been granted a conditional pardon, has violated the conditions subject to which such pardon has been granted. He further argues that even a rejection of evidence given by such persons at an earlier trial will not be an adequate ground

389

to charge them subsequently on the footing of a breach of the conditions; that the issue whether they gave false evidence either by suggestio Jalsi or suppressio very must itself be tried separately and decided against them in order that a trial for the original offence consequent on a non-compliance with the conditions of a conditional pardon can be justified. The reply to this submission by the Deputy Solicitor-General is that the Attorney-General alone has the power to decide this question and that it is not a matter for adjudication by a court. Three issues, therefore, arise for decision :-

(i) Whether the Attorney-General has the power to withdraw a conditional pardon tendered by him to any person, if he is of opinion, that such person has violated the conditions which attached to the pardon tendered, and to prosecute him for the original offence;

(ii) Whether the question of non-compliance with the conditions of the pardon is justiciable ; and

(iii) By which court it is to be decided.

In regard to the first two questions there is an absence of express provision in either the Criminal Procedure Code or the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act empowering the Attorney-General to withdraw a conditional pardon and proceed against the person who has accepted such pardon if, in his opinion, such person has not observed the terms of that pardon. We have, therefore, to decide whether the provisions relative to this matter confer this power on the Attorney-General by necessary implication.

The gist of Mr. Chitty's contention, which he has dealt with at considerable length, is that, when the Attorney-General tenders a conditional pardon to a person supposed to have been directly or indirectly concerned in or privy to an offence which is the subject of a charge in a particular case, he enters into a contract with that person. According to him, the words used in the relevant sections, namely, ' tender ', ' acceptance ', ' on the condition' and the documents which formed the basis of the agreement all support his contention. The essential condition of the contract, in his submission, is that the Attorney-General cannot prosecute the other contracting party for the original offence, if the latter makes a full and true disclosure of the whole of the circumstances within his knowledge relative to that offence and to every other person concerned, whether as principal or abettor in the commission thereof. Thereafter, one of the parties, namely, the Attorney-General, cannot decide unilaterally to launch on a prosecution of the other party without establishing the essential condition precedent, namely, that the latter has violated his part of the contract. Mr. Chitty's submission is that by no known principle of law can one of the parties be given the right to decide whether there has been a breach of the contract by the other party. This decision must be left to the court before the Attorney-General can take action by way of a prosecution. While we are not inclined to adopt the submission of Mr. Chitty that a conditional pardon

390

once accepted is a pure and simple contract, his argument that, ordinarily, in the absence of any specific provision to the contrary, a court should decide whether there has been a failure by the person pardoned to keep his undertaking, if the two parties are at issue as to whether there has been a breach by one party or not, does seem attractive.

The Deputy Solicitor-General submits on the other hand, firstly, that the tender and the acceptance of a pardon cannot be equated to a contract; secondly, that the power to withdraw the pardon on a breach of the condition is vested in the Attorney-General by Statute just as much as the power to tender the pardon is vested in him, and that that power cannot be questioned in a court of law, like most of the powers exercisable by the Attorney-General such as the power to enter a nolle prosequi, the power to re-open a non-summary inquiry and to commit an accused discharged by the Magistrate and the power to discharge an accused committed by the Magistrate. Alternatively, he submits that the court which tries the accused for the offence consequent on the violation of the condition of this pardon is the proper forum to inquire into the correctness of the withdrawal of the pardon and the exhibition of the Information.

After concluding his argument based on the relevant provisions of law, the Deputy Solicitor-General made a request to the court to permit Mr. Pullenayagam, Crown Counsel, to place before the court a certain aspect of the right of the Attorney-General to withdraw a pardon which he called the prerogative aspect and which, if his contention succeeded, would result in the decision of the Attorney-General being taken out of the sphere of Justiciability. Mr. Pullenayagam submitted that the power to withdraw such pardon was in the nature of or similar to the power to grant a pardon to an accomplice vested in the Governor-General by section 10 of the Ceylon (Office of Governor-General) Letters Patent, 1947, in the exercise of the Royal Prerogative. At the commencement of his submissions the power to tender a pardon given to the Attorney-General was almost equated by him to the exercise of a prerogative power. Subsequently, however, he dealt with the question on the lower basis of a power in the nature of a high prerogative power to support his argument. According to him, the power to grant pardons was vested with the Governor-General by section 10 of the Letters Patent and he exercises this prerogative power in the name of and on behalf of the Sovereign. All offences, he submitted, were against the Queen's peace and dignity and the power to grant a pardon is a special kind of prerogative power possessed by the Sovereign and, as the provisions contained in sections 283 to 286 of the, Criminal Procedure Code and section 11 of the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act also relate to a pardon-the accent being on the word ' pardon '-the Attorney-General must also be deemed to exercise a power in the nature of a high prerogative. His argument was that the prerogative of the Sovereign exercised through the Governor-General cannot be questioned in a court of law and similarly this power of pardon exercisable by the Attorney-General and the Attorney-General alone, which is in the nature of a prerogative power, cannot be questioned.391

He further argued that the power to enter a nolle prosequi and the power to grant a pardon are both unquestionable rights of the Attorney-General in the nature of the prerogative. He also emphasised that the Attorney-General was Her Majesty's Attorney-General-a description which is not reflected in the definition of the Attorney-General in the Criminal Procedure Code-the suggestion being that his exercise of the power to tender a pardon conferred by the Criminal Procedure Code was similar to or in the nature of the Sovereign's prerogative and cannot be questioned by a court. In answer to the questions put by the Bench he was content to reiterate that anything done in the exercise of the prerogative, which, according to Dicey was the residue of discretionary or arbitrary power in Her Majesty, was not justiciable by any court. This, we need hardly say, is a well established principle. He was not able to assist us with any authority in support of his submission. He relied on the fact that the word ' pardon ' was common to section 10 of the Letters Patent and to the relevant provisions of the Criminal Procedure. In this connection the observations of Lord Reid in the House of Lords in the case of The Burmah Oil Company Limited v. Lord Advocate [1 (1964) 3 W. L. R. 1231 at 1237.] are relevant. He describes the prerogative as " a relic of a past age, not lost by disuse, but only available to a case not covered by Statute." It is quite clear that the powers of the Attorney-General under sections 283 to 286 of the Criminal Procedure Code and section 11 of the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act are statutory. Counsel's endeavours, therefore, to vest the Attorney-General with powers of a high prerogative nature and to place him outside the pale of the courts is a submission unsupported by any principle of law and is wholly untenable.

The prerogative of Her Majesty has always been exercised in this country by Her representative, the Governor-General. The instrument, section 10 of which is relied upon for the submissions of the Crown, is described as the Letters Patent passed under the Great Seal of the Realm constituting the office of the Governor-General and Commander-in-Chief of the Island of Ceylon and its Dependencies. The only person on whom various powers are conferred by this instrument, some in the exercise of the prerogative and others not, is the Governor-General. The Criminal Procedure Code of Ceylon too has recognised some of the powers referred to in section 10 of the Letters Patent. Neither in the Letters Patent nor in the Criminal Procedure Code, which contains all the relevant powers exercisable by the Attorney-General, is he associated with the exercise of prerogative powers or anything in the nature of such powers. While acknowledging, therefore, the extraordinary powers vested by Statute in the Attorney-General by virtue of his high office, we are of the opinion that there is no warrant in law for this court to hold that the Attorney-General's power to tender a pardon is in the nature of a prerogative. In our view, there is a very great difference between the grant of a pardon in the exercise of the prerogative vested in the Governor-General by Letters Patent and the tender of a conditional pardon by the Attorney-General, for the purpose of obtaining evidence, in the exercise

392

of his statutory powers which tender is not complete without its acceptance. We also do not see, if the words ' grant' and ' tender' mean the same, why the tender of a pardon by the Magistrate in terms of section 283 of the Criminal Procedure Code should, on the submission of Crown Counsel, also not be called a power in the nature of a prerogative as, even here, though the Attorney-General's authority is first obtained, it is the Magistrate who tenders the pardon. We hold that all the powers of the Attorney-General, some of which are without doubt not questionable in a court of law, are circumscribed by Statute and that those powers have no relation or similarity to any prerogative powers of Her Majesty which are exercisable through the Governor-General. The courts are the watchdog of the liberty of the subject and have ever to be vigilant against any arbitrary or pretended use of prerogative powers and should be slow to accept any implied powers resting on the prerogative or anything in the nature of a prerogative.

A consideration and analysis of the relevant provisions of the Criminal Procedure Code and the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act alone are sufficient to determine the issue before us. The Deputy Solicitor-General and the counsel for the defendants were agreed that there has not been a single instance where an accused person has been brought up for trial for the offence in consequence of a non-compliance with the conditions of a pardon tendered by the Attorney-General. It behaves us, therefore, to treat this matter as res Integra and proceed to consider the question from first principles reminding ourselves that it is much more likely that accused persons were not brought to trial by the Attorney-General despite non-compliance with the terms of a conditional pardon rather than to proceed upon the unrealistic assumption that every accused person who was given a conditional pardon by the Attorney-General and made a witness thereafter divulged the whole truth in his evidence.

The first principle we have to bear in mind is the necessity to construe a Statute strictly where the construction results in the liability of a person to be tried on a criminal charge. The provisions under which the defendants mentioned in the present information have been tendered a conditional pardon by the Attorney-General is section 11 of the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act which is more or less an adaptation of section 283 of the Criminal Procedure Code with the necessary modifications to suit a Trial-at-Bar without a preliminary Magisterial inquiry. Sections 11 provides thus :-

" (i) In the case of any offence against the State the Attorney-General may, at any time before or after the commencement of the trial of that offence but before judgment is pronounced, with a view to obtaining on such a trial the evidence of any person supposed to have been directly or indirectly concerned in or privy to that offence, tender a pardon to such person, whether or not he is an accused, on the condition of his making a full and true disclosure of the whole

393

of the circumstances within his knowledge relative to that offence and to every other person concerned, whether as principal or abettor in the commission thereof.

(ii) The provisions of section 284 of the Criminal Procedure Code shall not apply in the case of any offence against the State but the other provisions of that Code relating to a pardon tendered under that section shall, mutatis mutandis, apply to a pardon tendered under sub-section (i) of this section in like manner and to the same extent as such other provisions apply in the case of a pardon tendered under the said section 284. "

It will be observed that sub-section (ii) of section 11 makes the relevant provisions of the Criminal Procedure Code relative to pardons applicable to a pardon tendered under section 11 of the Act. Copies of the two documents by which the pardon was tendered and accepted have been placed before us by the learned Deputy Solicitor-General and they are in the terms set out in document " B " [1 See page 404 (infra).]. It would appear that what the person pardoned undertakes to do by acceptance of the pardon is that he will make a full and true disclosure of the whole of the circumstances within his knowledge relative to such offence and to every other person concerned, whether as principal or abettor in the commission thereof. Section 285 of the Criminal Procedure Code provides thus :-

'' Where a pardon has been tendered under either of the last two preceding sections and any person who has accepted such tender has either by wilfully concealing anything essential or by giving false evidence not complied with the condition on which the tender was made, he may be tried for the offence in respect of which the pardon was so tendered or for any other offence of which he appears to have been guilty in connection with the same matter. "

This section clearly renders a person who, having accepted the tender of a pardon, either by wilfully concealing anything essential or by giving false evidence, has not complied with the conditions on which the tender was made, liable to be tried for the offence in respect of which the pardon was tendered. Section 286 (i) specifically refers to a withdrawal of the pardon under section 285. Such withdrawal must necessarily precede the institution of proceedings consequent on a failure to comply with the conditions of the pardon, and withdrawal will, in our view, be necessarily implied by the institution of proceedings without an express act of withdrawal. As conceded by Mr. Chitty too, that the Attorney-General is the competent person to withdraw the pardon tendered on a breach of the conditions thereof is, therefore, not in doubt. The important question, however, is whether the Attorney-General is the sole and final arbiter in this matter whenever the correctness of the withdrawal is raised in issue. In other words, is his decision final on the question whether there has been a breach of the conditions of the pardon or not,

394

whenever such an issue arises ? It is significant that there is no provision either express or implied, which makes the Attorney-General the sole and final arbiter on this matter. If the legislature intended to give him such a power it would have by the use of such words as " in the opinion of the Attorney-General" or words to that effect placed this matter outside the realm of argument. Indeed the legislature may well have provided that filing of an Information or the presentation of an indictment by the Attorney-General, consequent on a person's failure to comply with the conditions of his pardon, shall mean that all conditions required by law prior to the filing of such an Information or the presentation of such an indictment have been fulfilled. We are fortified in this view by the fact that there are other sections in the Criminal Procedure Code which utilise these words in order to vest the Attorney-General with unquestionable powers such as those dealing with the discharge or commitment of accused persons who have been either committed or discharged by a Magistrate after non-summary inquiry and the one relating to the presentation of indictments-vide sections 388, 391 and 165 (f) (i) of the Criminal Procedure Code.

To consider this matter from a practical angle, there may well be cases where a person arrested by the Police on the supposition that he was concerned in an offence makes a statement as desired by the Police under threats or duress. The factors that actuated the making of this statement will not necessarily be available to the Attorney-General, who may decide to tender a conditional pardon to that person. In court he may go back on this statement and speak the truth which results in an acquittal of the accused. The Attorney-General may take the view that the person concerned has given false evidence and has repudiated his undertaking. Is he, therefore, to be deprived of the opportunity of defending himself on the ground that he has not violated a condition attaching to the pardon ? Would it be reasonable in these circumstances to rely on the Attorney-General's opinion as being final on this question ? We think this is a question for the court to determine. It is only then that an accused or a defendant will have an opportunity to vindicate his position. The only reasonable conclusion we can come to in regard to the matter is that such words as were found in other sections of the Criminal Procedure Code which vest the Attorney-General with an unquestionable discretion were advisedly omitted from these sections. The contention of the learned counsel for the defendants, therefore, succeeds to the extent that, while the Attorney-General has the power to withdraw the pardon, the question whether there has been a breach of the conditions of the pardon or not, whenever it is raised by the defence, is one which, by its very nature, is justiciable by a court.

The next question which arises for consideration is which court is the proper forum to determine whether there has been a breach of the conditions on which the pardon was tendered and accepted.

395

Mr. Chitty contends that before an Information was laid in this case, the Attorney-General should have first armed himself with proof of a violation of a condition of the pardon and that this condition precedent being absent, this Information has been laid without warrant or authority. In regard to the aspect of false evidence his submission is that it is an offence triable by the District Court and that, therefore, the proper forum to which the Attorney-General should have gone in order to satisfy the first requirement before filing the Information is the District Court, which, according to the Criminal Procedure Code, is the only court which can take cognizance of this offence upon indictment. On the next question as to where the Attorney-General can seek a decision on the other aspect, namely, for wilful concealment of anything essential, Mr. Chitty does not have a satisfactory answer. For, there is no such offence as wilful concealment of anything essential known to our law. Mr. Chitty submitted, of course, that the absence of such an offence only shows that this is one of those situations not provided for in our law and that it will be wrong to assume that every situation is always provided for by law. He further submits that in a very large percentage of cases of non-disclosure of essential facts by a witness, it will amount to giving false evidence. He further argues if the law is carefully scrutinised there will be other rare instances of wrongs, in the nature of offences, which are not provided for and that we should not, therefore, reject his submission that there should be a conviction for giving false evidence in an appropriate court before an Information is laid, for the only reason that wilful concealment of anything essential is not an offence known to our law. While this is not the only criterion for our decision on this matter, we would say that if there had been such a provision in our law it might have tended to strengthen Mr. Chitty's contention just as its absence tends to weaken it. In this connection, it is, perhaps, relevant to pose the question whether the legislature would have intended such a cumbersome procedure requiring the Attorney-General, in whose opinion the pardoned person violated a condition of the pardon, to institute non-summary proceedings before the Magistrate's Court and thereafter to go through a successful trial in the District Court before laying the Information consequent on the breach. This seems to us unreasonable. Further, it is not an issue that cannot be more conveniently decided by this court which will be in possession of all the relevant material in the course of the trial. It seems to us, however, that the absence of any penal provisions in regard to non-disclosure of anything essential which is not cognizable as an offence by any court, strongly supports the view that the issue is one which can be raised and decided by this court like any other plea.

396

Mr. Chitty contended with some force that a trial of this issue by this court itself could cause serious prejudice to the defendants. We have given our anxious consideration to this submission. In considering this submission we remind ourselves of the observations of Swift, J. in Rex v. Essex Justices, exparte Perkins[1 (7927) 2 K. B. page 475 at 490.]:-

"It is essential that justice shall be so administered as to satisfy reasonable persons that the tribunal is impartial and unbiassed. "
The question here is whether the knowledge which a trial Judge gathers in the course of the proceedings which may be adverse to the person arraigned before him could reasonably be said to be prejudicial to a fair and impartial trial. The court which is trying the defendants is one consisting of three Judges who are members of the highest Tribunal of this country and considerations entirely different from those applicable to a Jury trial will apply to this court. Secondly, even where positive evidence of bad character is inadvertently led before a court presided by a trained Judge such as a District Judge or a Magistrate, the principle is that a conviction of an accused in such a case is not vitiated unless the Judge has been influenced by evidence of bad character. Thirdly, even a finding by this court that the defendants have violated a condition of the pardon, if this court eventually tries the defendants, would be irrelevant to the question whether or not they are guilty of the offences laid against them in the Information. Lastly, a trained Judge, even in courts of lower jurisdiction, is expected not to be prejudiced in the decision of a case even by his knowledge of the contents of a confession made by accused persons which is rejected by him on the ground of irrelevance or inadmissibility. For these reasons, we are unable to attach any weight to the argument resting on possible prejudice in support of the submission that the trial court is not the appropriate court to decide the issue in regard to the violation of the conditions of the pardon. We are also fortified in this view by the specific provision contained in section 339A of the Code of Criminal Procedure in India, which is quoted below, which makes it imperative for the trial court, in the event of the accused raising the plea that he has complied with the conditions on which the tender of the pardon is made, to determine in the same proceeding whether in fact he has complied with the provision or not. Although this provision is one that is absent in our law, the recognition in India of the principle embodied therein strengthens our view that this court is not precluded from trying both issues in the same proceeding. Section 339A of the Code of Criminal Procedure in India provides thus:-

" (1) The court trying under section 339 a person who has accepted a tender of pardon shall -

(A) if the court is a High Court or Court of Session, before the charge is read out and explained to the accused under section 271, sub-section (i) and

397

(B) if the Court is the Court of a Magistrate, before the evidence of the witnesses for the prosecution is taken,

ask the accused whether he pleads that he has complied with the conditions on which the tender of the pardon was made.

(2) If the accused does so plead, the court shall record the plea and proceed with the trial, and the Jury, or the court with the aid of the assessors or the Magistrate, as the case may be, shall, before judgment is passed in the case, find whether or not the accused has complied with the conditions of the pardon, and, if it is found that he has so complied, the court shall, notwithstanding anything contained in this Code, pass judgment of acquittal."

We accordingly hold that this court is the proper forum to decide the question whether there has been a breach of the conditions of the pardon.

Mr. Chitty also complained that the defendants had no notice whatsoever that they had gone back on their undertaking and that their pardon has been withdrawn. We are unable to find any provision of law which requires the Attorney-General to give any specific notice of this fact. In the absence of such a provision we do not feel justified in upholding this contention and deciding that such notice is a peremptory requirement. Even if such notice was necessary, we would say that when each witness went back in his evidence at the previous trial-as it was brought to our notice from the Bar-, when an application was made to the court in his presence to treat him as a hostile witness and when the Attorney-General proceeded to cross-examine him on the clear basis that he had gone back on the original statement, this was abundant notice to him that the Attorney-General considered him to have committed a breach of the condition on which a pardon was given. When this was followed by the service of the summons by this court containing the Information exhibited by the Attorney-General there could not have been any possible doubt in the minds of the defendants as to what the position was. Any further notice would, therefore, have been superfluous.

We now turn to a consideration of Mr. Chitty's submission that the entirety of the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act is invalid and that consequently the Information exhibited thereunder is bad in law. His argument is that the Act contains provisions which have abrogated or curtailed certain fundamental rights to which the defendants as citizens of this country are entitled, some of which are entrenched in the Constitution, and in regard to which Parliament cannot legislate.

Mr. Chitty enumerated the following objectionable features of the Act:-

(1) Depriving the defendants of their right to be tried by their peers ;

398

(2) (a) An uncontrolled power in the Inspector-General of Police to cause the arrest without warrant and to detain in any place, subject to his instructions, any person suspected to have committed an offence against the State ;

(b) The power granted to the Permanent Secretary to the Ministry of Defence and External Affairs to suspend or modify both the provisions of the Prisons Ordinance and the Rules made thereunder ;

(3) The deprivation of the liberty which any citizen has to have recourse to the court for release from unlawful detention ; and

(4) Creation of new offences making them retroactive.

In regard to trial by Jury, Mr. Chitty referred to section 3 of the  Criminal Law Act No. 31 of 1962 which repealed section 440A of the
Criminal Procedure Code and made certain offences triable by three Judges of the Supreme Court without a Jury; this provision, he submitted, is a breach of a fundamental right which dates back to Magna Carta, and is embodied in the Charter of 1833, and must be deemed, in the absence of provision to the contrary in the Constitution, to be the very fabric of our Constitution, and is, therefore, an entrenched provision which cannot be amended or repealed by an Act of Parliament. We need hardly observe that this Great Charter of liberty has not been attracted to our Constitution, though some of its terms find a place in our Statute book. Section 216 of the Criminal Procedure Code provides that all trials before the Supreme Court shall be by Jury before either a Judge or a Commissioner of Assize, or, if the Chief Justice in the exercise of his discretion so orders, a trial at bar by Jury before three Judges.
There is also statutory provision (section 440A of the Criminal Procedure Code) enacted about 50 years ago during a period of communal riots which empowered the Governor (and now in his place the Minister of Justice under our Constitution) to direct that certain offences against the State be tried before the Supreme Court at Bar without a Jury and we are aware that accused persons have been tried on such a direction. Mr. Chitty submits that this provision leaves untouched the right of an accused person to be tried by Jury in the case of capital offences such as treason and murder; but this distinction hardly helps the principle which he contended for, namely, that it is a right so fundamental that it must remain inviolate. For, this provision is a recognition by the Legislature even under a colonial system of Government of the principle  of trying accused persons without a Jury in respect of certain offences under certain circumstances. This change was, presumably, introduced into our law in the interests of accused persons since it may not have been possible to ensure that at a time of communal disturbance they will have a fair trial. The Legislature may, in the interests of good government, decide that in certain types of offences it is more desirable that accused persons should be tried at Bar without a Jury. While we

399

are not concerned with the motives of legislators, it may well be that in the case of offences against the State the Legislature in its wisdom considered it necessary to have such offences tried by three Judges of the highest Tribunal in this country without a Jury. The right which accused persons have, to be tried by Jury, is only in respect of offences triable before the Supreme Court, and not to numerous other offences, though of a grave nature, triable by any other court. Trial by Jury in our country is a statutory provision, a creation of the Legislature. The real question is whether this right is an entrenched provision of our Constitution. The Constitution of our country is contained in the Ceylon (Constitution) Order in Council, 1946 : there is no provision, either express or implied, which renders void or controls legislation in regard to trial by Jury. In the absence of such a provision we are of the view that it is within the power of Parliament to repeal or amend this statutory provision relating to trial by Jury, under section 29 of the Ceylon (Constitution) Order in Council.

In regard to Mr. Chitty's submission that there are certain provisions which violate the fundamental right of the defendants to seek redress from the Supreme Court to be released from unlawful custody, he contends that the defendants or anyone on their behalf will be prevented from applying to the Supreme Court for a writ of Habeas Corpus. It seems to us that this question does not arise for determination at the present stage of this inquiry and we do not propose to express our opinion in regard to this submission. Assuming, however, that these provisions do violate certain fundamental rights to which the defendants are entitled, and assuming further that these provisions are entrenched in our Constitution, we are of the opinion that their existence does not render the whole Act bad in law, because these provisions stand detached and are not inextricably woven into the pattern of the Act; the doctrine of severability, therefore, applies. The test was succinctly stated by Sulaiman, J. in the case of Shyamakant Lal. v. Rambhajan Singh et al.[2 A. T .R. 1939 (Federal Court) 74 at 83] in these words :-

" If the subject which is beyond the legislative power is perfectly distinct from that which is within such power, the Act can be ultra vires in the former while intra vires in the latter."

In Queen v. Liyanage et al .[. " (1962) 64 N. L. R. 313. ] this court would appear to have applied the test of severability to the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act and decided that, although section 9 was ultra vires the Constitution, section 8 was intra vires. We are of the opinion, assuming that the provisions referred to by Mr. Chitty are ultra vires the Constitution, the exclusion of it does not frustrate the whole Act.

Mr. Chitty's next submission is that charge 3 of the Information creates a new offence, which was not an offence under the law at the time the act was alleged to have been committed; he referred to the

400

amendment to section 115 of the Penal Code, by section 6 (2) (a) of the Criminal Law (Special Provisions) Act whereby the conspiracy to overthrow or attempt or preparation to overthrow the Government of Ceylon was made punishable. This Act had the assent of the Governor-General on 16. 3. 1962 but was made operative as from 1.1. 1962 ; the defendants were alleged to have committed this offence on 27. 1. 1962. The proposition that retroactive legislation is invalid is sought to be supported by Mr. Chitty on the principle that anything which is not forbidden by law is permitted. In other words, if at the time a person does an act. which does not offend against the law it is not an offence and cannot be made an offence subsequently. He sought recognition for this principle from section 19 of the Courts Ordinance which provides thus :-

" The Supreme Court shall have and exercise an original criminal jurisdiction for the inquiry into all crimes and offences committed throughout Ceylon."

It is not difficult to agree with the reasoning in this submission for every law that takes away or even impairs any existing rights from a period anterior to the passing of such law is by general standards unjust. Every law which with retrospective operation makes a penal offence of an act which was not considered to be an offence offends one's sense of justice. However, the principle that the Sovereign legislature has the undoubted power to pass retroactive or retrospective legislation is now so well established by a series of judicial decisions that a court will not be justified in accepting the contrary principle, however ably it may be supported. The principle has been recognised and acted upon in England for over a hundred years and our courts too have followed the principle. This is something that falls within the exclusive preserve of the legislature, and if the Act of Parliament which passed such a law has not contravened any limitation imposed on it in regard to that very matter no court can declare it to be bad. Craies on Statute Law (Sixth Edition) at page 388 sums up the position regarding the subject of retrospective legislation very effectively thus :-

" ' It is obviously competent for the legislature, in its wisdom, to make the provisions of an Act of Parliament retrospective.' ' No one denies,' said Dr. Lushington in The Ironsides, ' the competency of the legislature to pass retrospective statutes if they think fit, and many times they have done so.' Philosophical writers have, it is true, denied that any legislature ought to have such a power, and it is indisputable that to exercise it under ordinary circumstances must work great injustice. But ' before giving such a construction to an Act of Parliament one would require that it should either appear very clearly in the terms of the Act or arise by necessary and distinct interpretation.' And perhaps no rule of construction is more firmly established than this-that a retrospective operation is not to be

401

given to a statute so as to impair an existing right or obligation otherwise than as regards matter of procedure, unless that effect cannot be avoided without doing violence to the language of the enactment. If the enactment is expressed in language which is fairly capable of either interpretation, it ought to be construed as prospective only."

This subject is also dealt with in Alien's " Law in the Making " (Sixth Edition) at page 451. The author says in very forceful and categorical language:-

" There is, in English Law, no constitutional restraint uppn retroactive legislation and if an enactment is unequivocally expressed to operate retrospectively there is no power in the courts to derogate from it..........Whether or not Parliament chooses to legislate
retroactively is therefore a question not of the validity of statute law but of policy and statemanship ; and consequently the only de facto restraints which exist upon this kind of law are those which apply to all legislation - namely, wise Government and public opinion. "

This principle which is applicable to any country with a sovereign Parliament shows that if any retroactive legislation is abhorrent to the generally accepted standards of morality and justice the remedy lies in the hands of the people themselves whose representatives are responsible for the legislation. It is not the function of the courts to declare such legislation invalid so long as the legislation which embodies such provisions has been duly passed by the sovereign Parliament. It is the province of the legislature to decide on the existence or otherwise of any special circumstances, which call for any special legislation of a type that may be even out of place under normal circumstances. In the exercise of that function if the legislature decides to introduce laws which are retroactive and such laws are duly passed the courts are powerless to disregard them and their only function is to interpret them whenever the occasion arises. Indeed, as stated by Alien in his Book, there may be occasions when public exigency compels a departure from the general principle and it is impossible, therefore, to say that retrospective legislation is, in all circumstances, unjustifiable. The following observations of Willes, J. in the case of Phillips v. Eyre [1 (1870) L. R. "Q.R.1 at 27.] in this connection is both apposite and illuminating:-

" Allowing for the general inexpediency of retrospective legislation, it cannot be pronounced naturally or necessarily unjust. There may

402

be occasions and circumstances involving the safety of the State, or even the conduct of the individual subjects, the justice of which prospective laws made for ordinary occasions and the usual exigencies of society for want of prevision fail to meet, and in which the execution of the law as it stood at the time may involve practical public inconvenience and wrong-summum jus summa injuria."

This observation with which we are in respectful agreement proves the principle that the legislature may, in the interest of good government, consider that retrospective legislation is necessary to meet certain situations which may arise. For the court to assume the role of examining these aspects would be to misconceive its functions and to stray into the province of Parliamentary debate. For these reasons, we hold that, short of an Act of Parliament being invalid because an essential procedural step in its passage has not been complied with or because it has disregarded a prohibition or limitation placed on the legislative power of such Parliament, courts of law are bound to accept that law as passed by Parliament irrespective of whether it is prospective or retrospective.

These principles which have been the subject of judicial interpretation in England would equally apply to the Parliament of Ceylon. The only restriction placed upon the legislative power of Parliament in Ceylon is to be found in section 29 and, in a sense, to a limited extent in section 39 of The Ceylon (Constitution) Order in Council itself and so long as any Act of Parliament is duly passed and does not offend against restrictions placed in the sections referred to above, either expressly or by necessary implication, courts of law are obliged to treat such Acts of Parliament as having enacted good law, be it prospective or retrospective.

We may say that this matter was fully argued and received careful consideration by a Bench of three Judges of this court before whom the Trial at Bar No. 2 of 1962 was held and their rejection of the argument in regard to the invalidity of retroactive legislation fortifies the conclusion which we ourselves have reached. On an examination of a number of decisions of the English Courts we observe that this is one of those subjects in regard to which all the decisions have been in one direction, despite the almost universal natural revulsion of Judges towards the concept of retroactive laws.

403

The plea-in-bar raised by the defendants, therefore, fails and we direct that the defendants plead to the Information exhibited by the Attorney-General.
