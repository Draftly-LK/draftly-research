[COURT OF CRIMINAL APPEAL]

1966 Present : Sansoni, C.J., (President), Abeyesundere, J., and
Manicavasagar, J.

THE QUEEN v. R. P. D. JAYASENA

APPEAL No. 20 OF 1966, WITH APPLICATION No. 30

S. C. 94-M. G. Kuliyapitiya, 24302

Evidence-Commission of cognizable office -Investigation by police officer-Statement made to him by accused 'person-Discovery of facts resulting therefrom- Inadmissibility of oral evidence of Hat statement-Evidence Ordinance, as. 27, 9t-Criminal Procedure Code, s. 122 (3).

A portion of section 9J of the Evidence Ordinance reads aa follows :-

" .... in all cases' in which any matter is required by law to be reduced to the form of a document, no evidence shall be given in proof
of such matter, except the document itself, or secondary evidence of its contents in cases in which secondary evidence is admissible under the provisions hereinbefore contained."

Held, that inasmuch as a statement made to a police officer by any person in the course of an investigation under Chapter 12 of the Criminal Procedure Code must be reduced to writing, section 91 of the Evidence Ordinance debars any evidence being given of it except the document itself. Accordingly, if it is a statement falling under section 27 of the Evidence Ordinance, and any fact is deposed to as discovered in consequence of the statement, oral evidence of such statement is inadmissible.

APPEAL against a conviction at a trial before the Supreme Court.

T. S. P. Senanayake (Assigned), for the Accused-Appellant.

E. R. de Fonseka, Senior Crown Counsel, for the Crown.

Cur. adv. vult.

May 12, 1966. SANSONI, C.J.-

At the close of the argument, we allowed this appeal and acquitted the accused. We now give our reasons.

The accused was convicted, by the unanimous verdict of the Jury, of having committed rape on a girl called Podihamy and also of having murdered her.

The evidence against the accused was entirely circumstantial and consisted of the following items of evidence. A boy called Ariyadasa, who was about 15 years old, said that on the day in question, the 15th September 1964, he was fishing in a stream at about 4.30 p.m. The

370

accused came up to him, with a knife in his hand, and said " I killed the girl Podihamy. If you tell anyone else I will kill you also. Do not tell anyone." This witness was arrested on the following day on suspicion after the accused had been arrested. He knew that the accused had also been arrested. Although he made a statement to the Police he did not tell the Police anything about this alleged confession which had been made to him, nor did he mention it to his father. He seems to have been released very shortly after his arrest, but it was only on 2nd October that he first mentioned this to his father. His father took him to the Police and he then made a second statement, in which he mentioned the alleged confession. It is obvious from the summing-up of the learned Commissioner, and we agree with his view, that this item of evidence was most unreliable. It is hardly likely that the Jury would have accepted it.

The mother of the girl said that the girl left her house at about 4.30 p.m to go to the boutique carrying a mat bag with her, and that was the last time she saw the girl alive. About a quarter of an hour later she saw the accused going in the same direction, from his house which was on the adjoining compound. The accused was wearing a white soiled sarong, similar to the one which was produced in Court. According to the Inspector of Police that sarong was being worn by the accused when he was arrested on the following day at about 5.30 a.m.

Witness Simon, a brother-in-law of the girl's mother, said that he was walking in the direction of the same boutique at about 4 p.m. when he saw the accused squatting at a spot about one-eighth of a mile from the boutique. A little later he met the girl returning from the opposite direction, but he did not mention this latter fact to anybody until he gave evidence at the trial.

That there was enmity between the families of the accused and the girl is clear from the evidence of the girl's parents, and it was by reason of this enmity that the girl's father, when giving the Police the first information of this tragedy, said that he suspected the accused of having caused the death of his daughter.

The Inspector and a Police Constable said that they went to the accused's house in search of him early on the 16th morning at 5.15 a.m. When they were approaching it, they saw the accused on his verandah about 25 yards away. On seeing them the accused ran round to the rear of the house ; the Constable chased him and arrested him.

Apart from the alleged confession, these items of evidence which have been mentioned could hardly be said to establish the charges against this accused beyond reasonable doubt. It is true that the sarong which the Inspector says he found the accused wearing was examined by the Government Analyst and found to contain blood and seminal stains. But all these facts put together are insufficient to show that the accused had anything at all to do with the girl.

We have now to refer to one other item of evidence. The Inspector' said that he took the accused from his house to the place where the deceased's body had been taken out of a stream ; and about 100 feet away from that spot he recovered a mat bag. He added " The accused pointed out to me the spot where he dropped the mat bag ; then I went in that direction where the water was flowing and I found that bag ; that was about 25 yards away from the spot." The measured distance was about 100 feet down stream. This evidence was of an incriminating nature, because the bag was identified by the girl's mother when it was first shown to her in the Magistrate's Court, as the bag which the girl had taken with her that afternoon. At the trial the original bag was not: produced. Another bag was shown to the witnesses who said that it was not the bag which had been shown to them in the lower Court.

The question of law which arises is whether this statement, alleged to. have been made by the accused, was legally proved. Counsel for the' accused submitted that the only admissible evidence of the alleged statement is the record of it made by the Inspector. He further submitted that the accused had been prejudiced by the Inspector giving oral evidence of the contents of this statement. We uphold this submission. Without going further back into the authorities, we would refer to The King v. Haramanisa[1 (1944) 45 N.L.R. 532. ], where this Court held that by reason of section 91 of the Evidence Ordinance only the written record of a statement made to a Police Officer or Inquirer by any person in the course of any investigation under Chapter 12 of the Criminal Procedure Code is admissible in evidence, and that oral evidence of such a statement is inadmissible. A contrary view was taken in Rex v. Jinadasa[2(1950) 51 N.L R. S29.] which held that oral evidence of the statement of an accused falling under s. 27 of the Evidence Ordinance was admissible, and that section 122 (3) of the Criminal Procedure Code prohibits only the production of the written record of such a statement. In The Queen v. Murugan Ramasamy [3 (1962) 64 N.L.R. 433.] it was held that Jinadasa's case should not be followed ; and that neither the oral statement nor the written record of it made in the course of an inquiry under section 122 could be proved, even if it was a statement falling under s. 27 of the Evidence Ordinance.

The last word on this controversial subject has been spoken by the Privy Council in an appeal from that decision, (1964) 66 NLE.. 265. It overruled the decision of this Court and held that section 122 (3) prohibits the admission in evidence not only of the written record, but also oral evidence of anything said during an inquiry under Chapter 12, except in the case of a statement falling under section 27 of the Evidence Ordinance. No express decision was given on the question whether the written record or oral evidence of the statement should be led in evidence. But it was the written record that was produced in that case, and that appears to us to be the correct mode of proof in view of section 91 of the Evidence,

372

Ordinance. The relevant portion of that section reads : " in all cases in which any matter is required by law to be reduced to the form of a document, no evidence shall be given in proof .... of such matter, except the document itself, or secondary evidence of its contents in oases in which secondary evidence is admissible under the provisions hereinbefore contained." It was pointed out in The King v. Haramanisa that the statement made under section 122 of the Criminal Procedure Code must be reduced to writing, and hence no evidence can be given of it except the document itself. We would follow this decision.

It follows that the Inspector of Police should not have been allowed in the present case to give oral evidence as to what the accused told him in regard to the mat bag. It is a vital piece of evidence and there was no justification for a departure from the rule contained in section 91. If this evidence, which was illegally let in, is excluded, the prosecution has failed to establish the charges against the accused.

Accused acquitted.
