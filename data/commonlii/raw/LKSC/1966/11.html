Marikar Bawa Ltd v. Hanover Fire Insurance Co. of U. S. A. - NLR - 457 of 68 [1966] LKSC 11; (1966) 68 NLR 457 (25 February 1966)

457

1966 Present : H. N. G. Fernando, S.P.J., Abeyesundere, J.,
and G. P. A. Silva, J.

MARIKAR BAWA LTD., Appellant, and HANOVER FIRE INSURANCE
COMPANY OF U.S.A., Respondent

S. C. 419/1960-D. G. Colombo, 44,187\M

Insurance-Warranty-proposal form-Statements and promises made therein by the assured-Validity-Requirement of their clear incorporation in the policy. In a contract of insurance, statements and promises made by the assured in the proposal form prior to the issue of the policy of insurance are not operative as warranties unless they are incorporated into the contract. The proposal does not become part of the insurance policy unless it is expressly stated in the policy to be so, or unless it is expressed to be the basis of the contract.

The defendant (insurer) disclaimed liability upon a burglary policy of insurance on the ground that there had been a breach of warranty on the part of the plaintiff (the assured). The alleged warranty was set out in a declaration signed by the assured in the proposal form and was in the following terms :-

" I do hereby declare that the above answers are true and that I have withheld no information whatever regarding the proposal. I agree that this declaration and the answers above given shall be the basis of the contract between me and -------, and I further agree to accept a policy subject to the conditions in and endorsed on the policy."

The answers given to two questions in the proposal form were untrue. The policy of insurance commenced with the following recital:-

" Whereas Messrs Marikar Bawa Ltd. of No. 90 Chatham Street, Colombo (hereinafter called ' the Assured') is desirous of effecting an Assurance as hereinafter mentioned with Hanover Fire Insurance Company (hereinafter called ' the Company ') and has caused to be delivered to the Company a proposal or statement in writing dated the First day of June, 1956, signed by or on behalf of the Assured, warranting the truth of the statements therein contained, and has agreed that the said proposal or statement shall be the basis of this contract, and be considered as incorporated herein . . . ." Clause 10 of the insurance policy was as follows :-

" The observance and fulfilment by the Assured of the terms and conditions of this Policy and any endorsement which may be made hereon shall be a condition precedent to the Assured's right to recover hereunder ; and if the Assured, whether in the proposal aforesaid or in any statement made on any renewal of this insurance, or in connection with any claim hereunder, makes any misrepresentation, or intentional overstatement or intentional omission, the Policy shall be void, and all Premiums paid hereunder shall be forfeited."

Held (G. P. A. SILVA, J., dissenting), that, although the proposal contained the requisite agreement on the part of the assured, the policy itself did not duly incorporate the proposal or the agreement. There was neither a recital, nor any condition expressed in the policy itself, that the declaration in the proposal would in fact from the basis of the contract, nor was the proposal or a copy thereof attached to the contract. Accordingly, the policy was not void, and the insurer could not escape liability, on the ground of the untrue answers given by the assured to the two questions in the proposal form.

Held further (by the whole Court), that clause 10 of the policy, in view of its terms and the issues frame 1 and the evidence led in the case, could not be regarded as containing, in it' )lf, a warranty the breach of which could invalidate the policy.

APPEAL from a judgment of the District Court, Colombo.

H. V. Perera, Q.G., with S. J. Kadirgamar, Izadeen Mohamed, K, N. Choksy and D. G. Amarasinghe, for the Plaintiff-Appellant.

G. E. Chitty, Q.G., with N. Nadarasa, R. E. Nalliah and V. Basnayake, for the Defendant-Respondent.

Cur. adv. vult.

February 25, 1966. H. N. G. FERNANDO, S.P.J.-

The defence, successfully taken in the District Court, to this action upon a burglary policy was that the policy was void, or the insurer entitled to disclaim liability, on the ground that there had been a breach of warranty on the part of the assured. The alleged warranty was set out in a declaration signed by the assured on the proposal form Dl in the following terms :-

" DECLARATION :-I do hereby declare that the above answers are true and that I have withheld no information whatever regarding the proposal. I agree that this declaration and the answers above given shall be the basis of the contract between me and and I further agree to accept a policy subject to the conditions in and endorsed on the policy."

Date Signature of Proposer Sgd./..............Bawa Policy to commence from 1.6.56 for a period of 12 months ending........."

It can be assumed for present purposes that the trial Judge correctly held that the answers given to two questions in the proposal form were untrue. On that assumption, the decisive question is whether the policy sued upon expressly or by implication incorporated the warranty contained in the proposal or otherwise included such a warranty.

The policy commences with the following recital:-

"WHEREAS Messrs Marikar Bawa Ltd., of No. 90 Chatham Street, Colombo 1, (hereinafter called ' the Assured') is desirous of effecting an Assurance as hereinafter mentioned with Hanover FIRE INSURANCE COMPANY (hereinafter called ' the company') and has caused to be delivered to the Company a proposal or statement in writing dated the First day of June, 1956, signed by or on behalf of the Assured, warranting the truth of the statements therein contained, and has agreed that the said proposal or statement shall be the basis of this contract, and be considered as incorporated herein...."

459

There was here a reference to the agreement of the assured in his proposal that the declaration in the proposal shall be the basis of the contract, thus showing that the proposal operated as an inducement to the insurer to issue the policy. But there was neither a recital, nor any condition expressed in the policy itself, that the declaration would in fact form the basis of the contract, nor was the proposal or a copy thereof attached to the contract.

" Statements and promises in the proposal form may be warranties if they are incorporated into the contract. The proposal becomes part of the policy when it is expressly stated in the policy to be so or where it is expressed to be the basis of the contract." (Mac Gillivray, Vol. l-5th Edn., page 940.)

In my opinion the test just cited was not satisfied in the case of the policy in this case. Although the proposal contained the requisite agreement on the part of the assured, the policy did not incorporate the proposal or the agreement.


I have in this connection examined some of the cases mentioned in the foot-note appended to the passage cited above from Mac Gillivray.

Dawsons Ltd. v. Bonnin [1 (1922) 2 A. G. 413. ] and Hambrough v. Mutual Life Insurance Co.[2 12 L. T. 140.] were cases in which a declaration of warranty in a proposal had been incorporated in the policy by recitals respectively in the following terms:- " Which proposal shall be the basis of the contract and be held as incorporated therein ", and " In consideration of the application for this policy, which is hereby made a part of this contract''. These were clear incorporations of the proposal in the policy. In Finch v. Bannister, there had been a proposal coupled with a declaration of warranty, and a subsequent statement, expressed to be made with reference to the earlier declaration, but including no warranty; it was held that the subsequent declaration did not warrant the truth of the answers given in it. Macdonald v. Law Union [3L. R. 9 Q.B. 328.] was a case in which the policy itself contained a statement of warranty.

At the argument of the present appeal counsel for the insurer was not able to refer to any English decision holding that a mere reference to a proposal in a recital in the policy is equivalent to a clear formula of incorporation. But even if that is assumed to be so, such an assumption is negatived in the present case by the provisions of clause 10 of the policy:-

" The observance and fulfilment by the Assured of the terms and conditions of this Policy and any endorsement which may be made hereon shall be a condition precedent to the Assured's right to recover hereunder; and if the Assured, whether in the proposal aforesaid or in any statement made on any renewal of this insurance, or

460

in connection with any claim hereunder, makes any misrepresentation, or intentional overstatement or intentional omission, the Policy shall be void, and all Premiums paid hereunder shall be forfeited."

At the commencement of his argument, at this appeal, counsel for the insurer stated that clause ten merely set out the Common Law rule concerning misrepresentations and omissions on the part of an assured, which if material, would avoid a policy ; and he urged that the warranty relied upon arose on the proposal. But when met with the objection that the proposal had not been duly incorporated in the policy, he decided to rely on clause ten itself, arguing that the reference in the recital to the proposal, together with the reference to the proposal, in clause ten, had the effect that the policy and the proposal together formed the contract of insurance, of which the warranty in the proposal thus became a part. Counsel finally went to the further extreme of suggesting that, by itself, clause ten contained a warranty.

In the case of Dawsons Ltd. v. Bonnin (supra) there was, in addition to the incorporation of the proposal in the recitals, provision in the fourth clause of the policy that " material misstatement or concealment by the assured material to assessing the premium herein or in connection with any claim shall render the policy void. " The Law Lords were divided three to two, the majority holding that the presence of the fourth clause in the policy did not negative the intention that the warranty in the incorporated proposal should be fully effective. The approach which Viscount Cave made to the apparent difficulty arising from the existence of two different provisions for avoidance is of much assistance to me. He pointed out that the basis clause and the fourth clause do not cover the same ground, and that the condition (in the fourth clause) covers misstatements and concealments outside the proposal. It was on the footing of this distinction between the two clauses that Lord Cave disagreed with the minority opinion that the fourth clause should prevail over the basic clause.

The same distinction cannot be drawn in the present case, where clause ten is in terms applicable to statements in the proposal, and provides the penalty of avoidance only for misrepresentations and intentional omissions or overstatements. Clause ten therefore covers part of the same ground as would be covered by a warranty in a proposal. Mac Gillivray (paragraph 795) defines the essential characteristics of a representation as being '' that it makes a contract voidable if it is substantially false, material to the risk, and an operative inducement to contract ". The misrepresentations to which clause ten refers must be of that character, and that is sufficient reason to reject the ultimate suggestion that clause ten itself contains a warranty.

The argument that the proposal became part of the contract because of the two references to it in the policy requires some further examination. What is actually referred to in clause ten is not the proposal itself, but rather the statements in the proposal. In other words, the reference is to

461

a fact stated in or omitted from the proposal which may have amounted to a misrepresentation or non-disclosure. The reference is thus merely equivalent to an actual repetition in clause ten of all the questions and answers recorded in the proposal, and it is only those questions and answers and not any undertakings in the proposal which have to be read together with clause ten. If, for example, a motor vehicle policy contains clause which provides for avoidance in case of a conviction of a driver of any offences under the Traffic Laws, those laws do not thereby become incorporated in the policy, nor do those laws have to be read together with the Policy. The Policy's only concern would be with the fact of a conviction, to which the clause is intended to attach stated consequences.

The matters so far discussed enable me to reach an opinion on the remaining question of construction, which is whether the recital in the policy sufficed to incorporate the proposal and the agreement for a warranty which it contained. That question is answered upon a consideration of clause ten of the policy which clearly manifests an intention that the penalty of avoidance should attach to misrepresentation or nondisclosure, and not to all statements merely untrue in fact. The case before me has to be distinguished from Dawsons Ltd. v. Bonnins (supra) on two grounds :-There is no express incorporatioc of the proposal, and clause ten expressly contemplates only the application of the penalty of avoidance applicable under the Common Law, and not that applicable for breach of express warranty, for inaccuracy of answers stated in the proposal. The intention to rely on the breach of warranty is clearly negatived by the use in clause ten of the expression " misrepresentations or unintentional omissions or unintentional over-statements." I would hold that on the policy sued upon in this case the defence of breach of warranty did not arise, and could not have been pleaded.

At the trial of this action, the issues framed in defence, and which alone arose on the defence pleadings, were the following :-

5. (a) Was the writing marked A (which is the policy of insurance) issued on the faith of the proposal for insurance Dl and signed for and on behalf of the plaintiff ?

(b) Did the plaintiff thereby warrant the truth of the statements contained therein ?

(c) Was it agreed that the said proposal should be the basis of the contract of insurance contained in the said writing marked A and considered to be incorporated therein ?

6. Is the answer to question 5 (c) in the said proposal

(a) untrue ? and

(b) amount to misrepresentation ?

7. Is the answer to question 7 (a) in the said proposal

(a) untrue ? and

(b) amount to misrepresentation ?

462

8. If issues 5 (a) and/or 5 (b) and/or 5 (c) and/or Issue 6 and/or 7 are answered in the affirmative, is the policy of insurance sued upon

(a) void or

(b) is the plaintiff entitled to claim on the said policy ?

It is cleat from issue 5 that the insurer was depending on the warranties included in the proposal form to avoid liability. The omission to frame a specific issue upon the question whether the proposal had been duly incorporated in the policy would have been merely technical if in fact it was established that the proposal was thus incorporated. But the insurer's defence could not succeed because that fact was not established.

No issue was framed upon the basis that the policy was void in terms of clause ten on the ground of misrepresentation or intentional omission in the answers to questions 5 and 7 in the proposal form. Nevertheless the learned District Judge has recorded a finding that there had been in those answers material misrepresentations. It is unnecessary at this stage to consider the correctness of that finding.

It was urged that the finding for the insurer that the policy was void under clause ten should not now be disturbed because there was in fact evidence to justify such a finding. In this connection it is necessary to refer to the learned Judge's rejection of the evidence that the proposal form had been signed in blank by a Director of the Plaintiff Company. The first reason for rejection depended on some evidence of that Director which the learned Judge has cited in the judgment. After the Director had explained the circumstances in which (as he alleged) he signed the form in blank, there was the following question and answer recorded at the end of his cross-examination (this was only technical cross-examination because the Director had been called as a witness by the Defence):-

" Q. Did you even read the Proposal Form before you signed ?

A. The Secretary looked at it and he said that everything was alright and I signed. "

Immediately thereafter in re-examination, there was the following question and answer :-

" Q. You said after this Proposal Form was filled up you did not look - over it yourself but the secretary looked over it ?

A. He asked me to sign the Form."

On this material the learned Judge pointed out that " it was after the Secretary looked at the Proposal Form and told him (the witness) that everything was alright that he signed it ". The statement of the Judge as to the purport of the evidence was perfectly correct. But the inference he drew from it was that " if the form was being signed in blank there was nothing for the secretary to satisfy himself about by looking at the form before asking Mr. Bawa to sign it ". This inference was perhaps induced

463

by the question and answer in re-examination which has been reproduced above. That question carried the implication that the witness had previously admitted that the Secretary had looked over the Proposal Form after it had been filled up. In fact however no such admission had previously been made by the witness, whose position firmly had been that he had signed the Form in blank, meaning the blank printed form. Apart from this probable misconception, the learned Judge's apparent opinion that one cannot or would not " look over " a blank proposal form was also erroneous. A Proposal Form is not a blank piece of paper; and even if a proposer delegated to another the task of filling in the answers, there is nothing at all strange in his reading the printed questions before signing the form in blank. In this case, all that the Director meant to convey in his evidence was that he signed the blank form after his Secretary had perused what was already in print on that form. In so far as the Trial Judge depended on the evidence as to the circumstances of the signature to disbelieve the Director as to his signature in blank, that disbelief was not justified.

The Judge's second reason for disbelief on this matter was equally untenable. He thought that an experienced businessman like Mr. Bawa would not have set his signature to any document in blank, forgetting apparently the numerous reported cases which have presented difficulties of solution for the very reason that Insurance Proposals are quite often signed in blank by proposers, and thereafter filled up by other persons.

Thirdly, the learned Trial Judge was impressed by the omission of the Plaintiff Company, in correspondence with the insurer after the loss, to refer to their version of a signature in blank.

I doubt whether much reliance should have been placed on this omission, for without questioning the Plaintiff's lawyers it was unsafe to imply from the omission that the evidence of signature in blank was an after thought. In view of the Case law holding that ordinarily the act of an insurance agent in filling up a proposal form would bind the proposer, there is much apparent reason for the omission of the Plaintiff's lawyer to refer in the correspondence to this matter of signature in blank.

In all the circumstances therefore it was unreasonable for the Trial Judge to hold as he did that the Proposal had been completed before signature. In the absence of any direct evidence to contradict the testimony of Mr. Bawa that he signed the form in blank, that testimony should in my opinion have been accepted.

For the reasons just stated I hold that the Plantiff did establish that the Proposal had been signed in blank. Ordinarily, that circumstance would not have been of much avail against the plea that some particulars thereafter entered in the form constituted misrepresentations material to the risk. But the facts of this case were unusual in that the plaintiff company had direct contact with the Manager in Ceylon of the insurance company, who apparently had full authority to decide whether or not to issue a policy without reference to his principals abroad. This Manager was not a witness at the trial, nor was the person who has hitherto been referred

464

to as " the secretary " of the plaintiff company called as a witness ; that person was in fact one who performed for the plaintiff company, as also for other companies, the functions of a secretary for the purposes of the Company Law. The evidence as to the identity of the person who actually filled up the form is inconclusive, and it is only a fair assumption that this secretary supplied, on behalf of the plaintiff, the answers to questions in the Proposal Form. Nevertheless, if the plea of material misrepresentation had been expressly taken and agitated at the trial, it might have been met successfully by further evidence tending to show that the challenged representations were not in fact material, or that the Manager had been responsible for framing the answers to the questions in the proposal, or that the statements in the proposal had not in fact been an operative inducement for the Manager to issue the policy.

The hand-writing at the top of the form, particularly the writing in which is entered the Number of the policy ultimately issued, much resembles the other writing in the form. That circumstance might have assisted the plaintiff, with other evidence, to establish one or other of the answers available to meet a defence of material misrepresentation.

It is an unusual feature in this case that it was the insurer's Manager and not a mere agent or a canvasser, who had dealt with the plaintiff company. There is on the evidence reason to suppose that the Manager himself regarded the proposal as a formality and that his decision to issue the policy was influenced substantially by his own knowledge of the manner in which establishments such as the one whose stock in trade he insured in this case were in fact maintained. There is also the circumstance that the proposal form was signed in blank (as I hold), and doubt as to who filled up the form. The Manager's lack of concern as to the proposal is shown by the fact that in the declaration, there were left blank the spaces intended for the insertion of the name of the insurer and of the date of signature ; in addition the recital in the Policy that the assured had agreed in his proposal for the incorporation of the warranty is incorrect, for the declaration does not refer to that matter. In these circumstances it is not at all clear that, even if the answers to the two questions in the proposal form which were challenged as untrue had been correctly stated, the Manager would have declined the proposal risk or demanded a higher premium.

I am satisfied that the issues framed at the trial did not give the plaintiff fair warning of the plea of material misrepresentation arising on clause ten of the policy, and that, if such a plea had been put in issue, further evidence might have been adduced to meet it. It appears that the Defendant Company's Manager had left this country before the trial, but the cost of procuring his attendance as a witness should not have been prohibitive for the plaintiff Company if the need of his evidence has been made apparent.

I would hold for these reasons that the insurer failed to establish grounds of avoidance of liability under the Policy.

465

No argument was adduced to us as to the correctness of the finding of the trial Judge that, if the insurer is liable to meet the plaintiff's claim, he would be liable to meet the full amount of that claim.

In the result I would allow this appeal, and order decree to be entered for the plaintiff as prayed for in the plaint and for costs in both Courts.

ABEYESUNDERE, J.-I agree.

G. P. A. SILVA, J.-

While agreeing with the conclusion reached by my brother Fernando, I would prefer to approach this case from a different angle.

The proposal form which preceded the Insurance Policy on which this action was founded contains at the end of it the following declaration :-

" I do hereby declare that the above answers are true and that I have withheld no information whatever regarding the proposal. I agree that this declaration and the answers above given shall be the basis of the contract between me and THE MARIKAR BAWA LTD., and I further agree to accept a policy subject to the conditions in and endorsed on the policy." At the beginning of the Policy the following recital appears :-

"WHEREAS Messrs Marikar Bawa Ltd. of No. 90 Chatham Street, Colombo 1 (hereinafter called " the assured ") is desirous of effecting an Assurance as hereinafter mentioned with Hanover FIRE INSURANCE COMPANY (hereinafter called "the Company") and has caused to be delivered to the Company a proposal or statement in writing dated the First day of June, 1956, signed by or on behalf of the Assured, warranting the truth of the statements therein contained, and has agreed that the said proposal or statement shall be the basis of this contract, and be considered as incorporated herein."

When one considers the parties concerned, the chronological order of the events culminating in this Policy of insurance, and the fact that the language of the proposal attracts the Policy and that of the Policy the proposal, it would appear that both these documents must necessarily be considered together as forming one contract of insurance and that the material disclosed in the proposal constituted the required information which resulted in the sale of the Policy. The words used in the two documents are sufficient in my view to incorporate the proposal in the final contract of Insurance. Thus the principle that the proposal becomes

466

part of the Policy when it is expressly stated in the Policy to be so or where it is expressed to be the basis of the contract, as enunciated by McGillivray and referred to by my brother in his judgment, is, I think, broadly answered in the affirmative in the instant case. It is therefore necessary to consider the question whether the defendant is absolved from liability on the basis of a breach of warranty on the part of the plaintiff. McGillivray goes on to say at the end of the passage quoted by my brother in paragraph 940 " If the proposal is incorporated all express warranties and declarations therein will have the same force as if they were contained in the body of the Policy and the presumption that all obviously material statements and promises contained in the Policy are warranties applies equally to the proposal if made part of the Policy". This would mean that all representations made in the proposal form which is part of the contract do not necessarily become warranties. In order to become warranties they must be expressly stated to be so. In the proposal form under consideration there is no-such express warranty. The declaration in the proposal which I have already quoted seems to me to fall short of any express warianty as contemplated above. Even if the declaration gives the facts stated in the answers to questions the character of warranties, clause 10 of the Policy has the effect of excusing an inadvertent error and of taking such an error outside the pale of warranties.

The insurer cannot therefore escape liability on the ground of a breach of warranty in that there are two errors in the answers furnished by the plaintiff in regard to valuables and unoccupied hours. A somewhat analogous situation appears to have been contemplated in the view expressed in a Canadian case referred to by McGillivray in paragraph, 941 where it was held that when a clause in the Policy provided that in the absence of fraud all declarations made by the assured should be considered as declarations and not as affirmations, the incorporation of the proposal and the answers to the questions therein into the Policy did not convert them into warranties and notwithstanding such incorporation they remained representations only.

The question however remains for consideration whether on the basis of clause 10 of the Policy alone, apart from any warranty, the defendant company can escape liability to meet the claim of the plaintiff by reason of the two erroneous answers given to certain questions in the proposal form. For reasons which I set out below I think that the entire question of rights and liabilities in this case revolves round clause 10 which provides:-"The observance and fulfilment by the Assured of the terms and conditions of this Policy and any endorsement which may be made hereon shall be a condition precedent to the Assured's right to recover hereunder ; and if the Assured, either in the proposal aforesaid or in any statement made on any renewal of this insurance, or in connection with any claim hereunder, makes any misrepresentation, or intentional overstatement or intentional omission, the Policy shall be void, and all premiums paid hereunder shall be forfeited." According

467

to this clause, the condition precedent to the plaintiff's right to recover under the Policy is " The observance and fulfilment by the assured of the terms and conditions of this Policy and any endorsement which may be made hereon ". It cannot be said that the assured in this case, namely the plaintiff, has failed to observe or fulfil any of the terms and conditions of the Policy to be distinguished from the proposal. Prima facie therefore the plaintiff has a right to recover unless he has according to the same clause, either in the proposal aforesaid or in any statement made on any renewal of this insurance, or in connection with any claim under the Policy, made any misrepresentation or intentional overstatement or intentional omission in the event of which the Policy will be void. These transgressions, if I may use that term, would therefore appear to be in the nature of exceptional circumstances which would render the Policy void and thereby preclude the insured from advancing any claim under the Policy. Implicit in the wording of this exception is a statement that any misrepresentation which is not deliberately erroneous or an accidental or inadvertent overstatement or omission does not render the Policy void. I am not unmindful of the omission before the word misrepresentation of a limiting adjective which found a place before the words " overstatement" and " omission " which immediately followed. I however feel confident in my mind, having regard to the context and the association of these three words, that what is contemplated is something more than a misrepresentation, an overstatement or an omission simpliciter ; it is a misrepresentation, an overstatement or an omission which involves a certain mental element and which is calculated if not to mislead the insurer, at least to result in some gain to the insured. I cannot say that the evidence in this case in any way justifies a conclusion in favour of the existence of such a mental element on the part of the insured or that any error-which in this instance can reasonably be characterised as inadvertent-has had any effect or made any difference to the insurer in regard to the transaction culminating in the issue of this Policy. To my mind clause 10 of the Policy has the effect of qualifying the declaration in the proposal form and reduces the force of the affirmation to the extent that any erroneous answer or information is excused unless it can be shown to be a misrepresentation, an intentional overstatement or an intentional omission of the type I have referred to.

In this connection it is useful to examine the main items of evidence which have a bearing on the Policy. Bawa, a Director of the plaintiff company discussed the matter of the Insurance Policy with one Bias, the agent of the defendant and Peiris who acted as Secretary for the plaintiff company as well as several others. When Bawa expressed a desire to insure for three lakhs, Dias said it was not necessary and that it would be sufficient if the insurance covered a loss of one lakh. Thereafter Dias got a proposal form signed by Bawa in blank and left him and at the most Peiris and Dias would have filled in the necessary particulars in consultation with each other. On the following day Dias and Peiris both came with the Policy, which must obviously have been brought

468

by Dias, and collected the premium cheque from Bawa. An examination of the form discloses that the following cages which require to be fully perfected and can be perfected have not been done :-

(1) 4 (b) remains unanswered.

(2) the second question in 5 (a) is not answered,

(3) 7 (6) (5) is not answered.

(4) 11 (a) and (b) are not answered.

The Policy itself, which would normally be issued after a reasonable interval after the proposal is handed over and after full consideration by the principals has been delivered in this instance on the very next day by Dias who functioned as Agent, even before the premium had been paid by the plaintiff. This obviously shows that the principals were not even consulted and that Dias functioned in loco principles and had the authority to do so. There is also some evidence that Dias operated from Hirdaramani's building situated in the same street as, the plaintiff's place of business from which fact it is reasonable to draw the inference that Dias knew the hours and days on which shops on that street remained closed and unoccupied. All these circumstances tend to show that the proposal form was considered a mere formality, that it was perhaps filled by the agent who also acted as the principal or at least in consultation with him and that this agent did not even care to see that all the questions which should have been answered in the proposal were answered before the Policy was issued. In this view of the facts, quite apart from the legal position, there would be little justification for holding the plaintiff liable for any representations or omissions for which he was not in fact responsible. Even if the plaintiff be held responsible on the basis that his Secretary, as his agent was associated with the agent of the Insurers in filling the particulars of the proposal form, can it be concluded in this case that the failure to state that some cheap jewellery was not secured in the burglar-resisting safes and the omission to state that the premises remained unoccupied all day on Sundays constituted a misrepresentation or an intentional omission of the class which I have discussed earlier ? In my view the answer to this has to be in the negative. I can see no reason why the plaintiff should make such a misrepresentation or an intentional omission nor can I say that the plaintiff company is the type that would resort to such a practice in bad faith, having regard to the high tributes paid to their business integrity by the Surveyor who was employed by the defendant Insurer to survey and assess the loss resulting from the burglary.

These are the main reasons which persuade me to the conclusion that the appellants must succeed in their appeal

Appeal allowed.
