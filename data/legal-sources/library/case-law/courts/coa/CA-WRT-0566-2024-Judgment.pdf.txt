Introduction The Petitioner is the Chartered Institute of Personnel Management incorporated by the Institute of Personnel Management, Sri Lanka Law No, 24 of 1976, as amended by Act No. 31 of 2018 (“the CIMP Law”). In the instant application, the Petitioner has impugned the order of the 1 st Respondent Right to Information Commission consisting of the 2nd to 5th Respondents dated 08.08.2024 (P1) holding that the Petitioner is a “public authority” in terms of section 43 of the Right to Information Act, No. 12 of 2016 and has sought writs of Certiorari to quash such order in toto or the part thereof relating to the aforementioned finding. The 5A Respondent, who had submitted a request for information from the Petitioner and had lodged an appeal before the 1st Respondent Commission (“the RTI Commission”) in view of the failure on the part of the Petitioner to respond to the said request, had been permitted by Court to intervene in these proceedings. The 1st to 5th Respondents as well as the 5A Respondent, by way of their statements of objections, have objected to the grant of the aforementioned reliefs sought by the Petitioner. Accordingly, the application was taken up for argument on 27.04.2026 and the parties have also filed pre-and post-hearing written submissions. The 5A Respondent has also raised certain legal objections, which I intend to address in due course after a consideration of the merits of the application. We do not find this contention tenable. In our view, a reading of the Sinhala and English versions of Section 43 (b) of the RTI Act does not give rise to a 'discrepancy' or an 'inconsistency' between the two versions as the Respondent contends. Rather, the Sinhala version of ‘uKav,h’ is a linguistic translation of the term ‘anybody' (English version of Section 43 (b)). Regarding such conflicting and competing positions taken up by the parties, in the first instance, I wish to observe that the dispute is confined to the issue whether the term “any body” is an accurate translation of the term ‘uKav,h’, since there appears to be no divergence of opinion between the parties that the term “office” is an accurate translation of the term “ld¾hd,h.” Secondly, I wish to observe that the actual term used in the Act for the term ‘uKav,h’ in the English text is “any body,” and, as such, the Petitioner is inviting this Court to replace the said term “any body” with the term “Board” in the English text, in interpreting the ambit of section 43(b). On this issue, the 1st to 5th Respondents have specifically taken up the position that the Courts cannot be invited to substitute statutory language in determining whether or not a provision of a law is too broad, and, that the Courts are bound to give effect to, and interpret legislation according to the plain, ordinary and literal meaning of the words employed in the statute irrespective of the result, if the words of the statute in question are clear and unambiguous. In advancing such position, the said Respondents have adverted to the following citation in the celebrated text Maxwell on The Interpretation of Statutes10 relating to literal construction; The rule of construction is “to intend the legislature to have meant what they have actually expressed.” The object of all interpretation is to discover the intention of Parliament, “but the intention of Parliament must be deduced from the language used,” for “it is well accepted that the beliefs and assumptions of those who frame Acts of Parliament cannot make the law.” Where the language is plain and admits of but one meaning, the task of interpretation can hardly be said to arise. … Where, by the use of clear and unequivocal language capable of only one meaning, anything is enacted by the legislature, it must be enforced, however harsh or absurd or contrary to common sense the result may be. The interpretation of a statute is not to be collected from any notions which may be entertained by the court as to what is just and 10 P. St. J. Langan, Maxwell on The Interpretation of Statutes (12th ed, Sweet & Maxwell, 1969) pp 28-29 11 expedient; words are not to be construed contrary to their meaning, as embracing or excluding cases merely because no good reason appears why they should not be embraced or excluded. The duty of the court is to expound the law as it stands, and to “leave the remedy (if one can be resolved upon) to others." I am inclined to accept the position taken up by the 1 st to 5th Respondents that it would not be appropriate for this Court to make any substitutions to the statutory language used in the English text of section 43(b), specifically to replace the term “any body” with the term “Board”, as the Petitioner has invited this Court to do. Thus, I am of the view that section 43(b) should be interpreted with recourse to the specific text used in the Act without any modification. Thirdly, I wish to observe that it was not submitted by any party that a strict word-by-word translation has to be done in translating legislation. Neither does there seem to any legislative sanction or judicial decisions supporting such an approach. Next, I wish to consider the vires and the reasonableness of the interpretation accorded by the RTI Commission to the ambit of section 43(b). In the order (P1), the Commission has made the following findings on the ambit of section 43(b); We are of the view that sub-section (b) is a singular limb of Section 43 that stands on its own clearly untrammeled by the conditional contexts of the other subsections of Section 43. That distinction between Section 43(b) and other relevant sub-sections of Section 43 will be adverted to later. In other words, the only consideration applicable for Section 43(b) to operate will be whether ‘any body’ or ‘office’ has been created or established by ‘the Constitution or any written law…’ Consequently we find that the Respondent CIMP falls firmly and squarely within the ambit of a ‘Public Authority’ in terms of Section 43(b). In their post-hearing written submissions, the 1 st to 5th Respondents has identified section 43(b) as a “broad and all- encompassing provision” that covers any entity that is incorporated by an Act of Parliament (as in the case of the Petitioner Institute), bringing them within the purview of the RTI Act.” 11 On the contrary, the Petitioner has advocated a more restrictive interpretation, contending that the application of section 43(b) should be limited to “Boards” or “Offices” established by or under any written law, and, has alleged that such expansive meaning accorded in the RTI order (P1) to the term “anybody” in the English translation has had the effect of obliterating the distinction between such terms contemplated in the Sinhala text. The response of the RTI Commission to such contention, as found in the order (P1), is that such an interpretation would “seriously confine the application and ambit of the RTI Act” and that would lead to an “absurd result” where the RTI Commission itself would be excluded from the application of the Act. The 5A Respondent too has contested the above interpretation advanced by the Petitioner and submitted that only companies incorporated under the Companies Act No. 7 of 2007 (as 11 Post-hearing written submissions of the 1st to 5th Respondents, paragraph 10 12 amended) are the only “bodies” and “offices” established by the Constitution or any written law that have been exempted from the application of section 43(b). The 5A Respondent has sought to justify such conclusion upon the bases that the Legislature has (a) excluded companies incorporated under the Companies Act, as they are not “Boards” or “Offices” other than companies specified in sub-paragraph 43(e) of the Act; (b) included companies incorporated under the Companies Act in which the State or a public corporation or both together hold 25% or more of the shares or otherwise has a controlling interest, as specified in section 43(e); and (c) included a Government Department or a public corporation under sections 43(c) and 43(d) respectively. As reflected in its post-hearing written submissions, in concluding that the Petitioner fell within the ambit of section 43(2), the RTI Commission appears to have placed heavy reliance upon two factors; one being the fact of the Petitioner’s establishment and incorporation by an Act of Parliament; and the other being the public nature and significance of its activities.12 It is observed that incorporation by an Act of Parliament denotes and confers the imprimatur of Parliament regarding its significance, powers and functions of such institution. In my view, it is the highest status and form of recognition that a body corporate can acquire and cannot be easily disregarded. It is also not irrational or unreasonable to expect such an institution to conduct its affairs in a transparent manner and be accountable for its actions, which are the objectives that the regulatory regime on the right to information seeks to foster. On the second factor, the submissions made by the parties in respect of the functions and activities of the Petitioner have been discussed in the RTI order (P1), whilst reference has also been made in its written submissions to the public nature and significance of the objects of the Petitioner in section 3 (a), (c), (d), (e), (f) and (g) of the CIPM Law. Considering the conflicting and competing positions taken up by the Petitioner and the 5A Respondent before it, I am of the view that the use of such criteria by the RTI Commission to determine whether a particular institution can be considered as a “public authority” in terms of section 43(2) is rational and reasonable. Since all parties have referred to the intention of the Legislature, I propose to examine such issue with recourse to relevant statutory provisions and judicial decisions. The Preamble to the RTI Act articulates the object and the purpose of the Act in the following terms; WHEREAS the Constitution guarantees the right of access to information in Article 14A thereof and there exists a need to foster a culture of transparency and accountability in public authorities by giving effect to the right of access to information and thereby promote a society in which the people of Sri Lanka would be able to more fully participate in public life through combating corruption and promoting accountability and good governance. (emphasis added) 12 vide paragraph 38 13 Such intention of the Legislature has been referred to in several decisions of our Superior Courts. When the constitutionality of the Right to Information Bill was challenged, the Supreme Court, in its Determination has, inter alia observed as follows;13 The express recognition of the right as enshrined in Article 14A undoubtedly advances the sovereignty exercised and enjoyed by the people and accords with the Constitutional directive postulated in Article 4 (d) of the Constitution, which requires that “the fundamental rights which are by the Constitution declared and recognized shall be respected, secured and advanced by all organs of government and shall not be abridged, restricted or denied, save in the manner and to the extent hereinafter provided”. (emphasis added) Accordingly, the right of access to information like all other rights recognized under the Constitution should also be enjoyed subject to certain overriding public interests in non￾disclosure as well as the constitutional duties stipulated under Article 28 (e) to the Constitution, where it is specifically stipulated that the exercise of and enjoyment of rights and freedoms are inseparable from the performance of duties and obligations and accordingly it is the duty of every person in Sri Lanka to respect the rights and freedoms of others. Hence, Clause 5 of the Bill in accordance with Article 14 (A) (2) of the Constitution performs and equally important task in ensuring that the right of access to information is suitably restricted to reflect these important countervailing considerations. Therefore, the underlying approach of the Bill is to meaningfully give effect to the right of access to information enshrined in Article 14A(1) while successfully balancing such right with restrictions that are necessary in a democratic society as set out in Article 14A (2). Legal Objections The 5A Respondent has raised two preliminary objections in respect of the instant application. Although the consideration of such legal objections is not necessary in view of my aforementioned findings on the merits, for the sake of completeness, I will endeavour to briefly address such objections. A. Necessary Parties The 5A Respondent has pointed out that the Petitioner had filed and supported the instant application without citing the said 5A Respondent as a party, and, after obtaining notice, had belatedly sought to add the said party as a respondent to cure a fundamental defect in the institution of this application.17 It is the position of the 5A Respondent that, as the successful party before the Commission and as a party whose rights would be affected by an order made in these proceedings, the said 5A Respondent is a necessary party who should have been mandatorily cited as a party from the very inception in this application. In addition, the 5A Respondent has alleged that such conduct on the part of the Petitioner demonstrates mala fides, procedural impropriety and an abuse of the process of Court. The response of the Petitioner to the 5A Respondent had been added at a very early stage before any formal proceedings requiring the presence of all parties had commenced and as such no prejudice had been caused to him.18 I am unable to accept such position taken up by the Petitioner. In the instant application, the Petitioner had impugned an order made by the RTI Commission in the 5A Respondent’s favour, and, was fully aware or could reasonably be expected to be aware that that the relief sought in the instant application, if granted, would 17 Written submissions of the 5A Respondent, paragraph 3 18 Post-hearing written submissions of the Petitioner, paragraphs 139-143 16 adversely affect the rights and interests of the said party. As such, the 5A Respondent was not merely a necessary but an essential party to the instant application, and, should have been cited as a party when the application was instituted. I further observe that no reasons have been adduced by the Petitioner for the failure to cite the 5A Respondent as a party at the time the instant application was instituted, and, as such, I am inclined to believe that none exists. I also observe that, by not being cited as a party from the very inception of this application, the 5A Respondent has been precluded from objecting to the issuance of formal notice. Hence, it cannot be stated that no prejudice has been caused to the said Respondent, as alleged by the Petitioner. In view of the aforementioned circumstances, I uphold the objection raised by the 5A Respondent that the Petitioner has failed to cite necessary and material parties and further hold that the belated addition of the said Respondent does not cure such defect. I am guided by the reasoning in Jamila Umma v. Mohammed, 19 cited by the 5A Respondent in his written submissions, in arriving at this conclusion. B. Alternative Remedies The 5A Respondent has contended that the Petitioner has failed to exhaust the available statutory remedy and has improperly invoked the writ jurisdiction of this Court. He has pointed out that, without availing itself of the statutory right of appeal to this Court conferred by section 34 of the RTI Act, the Petitioner has filed the instant application invoking the writ jurisdiction of this Court. Section 34 of the RTI provides for an appeal to the Court of Appeal from a decision of the RTI Commission in the following manner; 34. (1) A citizen or public authority who is aggrieved by the decision of the Commission made under section 32, may appeal against such decision to the Court of Appeal within one month of the date on which such decision was communicated to such citizen or public authority. (2) Until rules are made under Article 136 of the Constitution pertaining to appeals under this section, the rules made under that Article pertaining to an application by way of revision to the Court of Appeal, shall apply in respect of every appeal made under subsection (1) of this section. The Petitioner’s response to this objection is that the impugned order (P1), in which the RTI has overruled the preliminary objection raised by the Petitioner and set the 5A Respondent’s appeal down for hearing on the merits, is not a decision falling within the ambit of section 32 of the RTI Act from which an appeal is available under the aforementioned section 34 of the Act.20 The Petitioner further submits that the statutory right of appeal contemplated under section 34 is “expressly and exclusively confined” to decisions made by the Commission under 19 50 NLR 15 at 17 20 Post-hearing written submissions of the Petitioner, paragraphs 19-30 17 section 32 of the said Act, where the Commission has either affirmed, varied or reversed the decision appealed against and forwarded the request back to the information officer concerned for necessary action. Thus, the Petitioner has contended that invocation of the writ jurisdiction of this Court under Article 140 of the Constitution is not only available but is the proper and appropriate remedy. It is observed that the right of appeal accorded in section 34 of the RTI Act appears to be in respects of “decisions” of a final nature, and, viewed from such perspective, the impugned order (P1), which essentially has the characteristics of an “interlocutory order,” does not have such effect. Hence, I am inclined to accept the position taken up by the Petitioner that the writ jurisdiction of this Court is available to impugn the order (P1) (of course, subject to the Petitioner establishing its entitlement to the reliefs sought). Accordingly, I reject the objection raised by the 5A Respondent relating to alternative remedies. Conclusion and Orders of Court For the reasons set out above, I hold that the Petitioner has failed to establish its entitlement to the reliefs prayed for the petition. The legal objection raised by the 5A Respondent relating to material parties, as determined by me, too militate against the grant of prerogative relief. Therefore, I proceed to dismiss the Petitioner’s application. The parties will bear their own costs. Application dismissed. Judge of the Court of Appeal Mayadunne Corea, J. I agree.
