## Original background

A deed of gift states that it is absolute and irrevocable.

## M039-Q01 (enrich, hop count 2)

### Question

Could it be revoked?

### Enriched background

Wimalasena Perera, aged 74, a retired schoolmaster of Panadura, gifted the land called Kahatagahawatta, thirty-two perches in extent with the house standing on it, at Panadura in the District of Kalutara, to his nephew Chaminda Perera. The parties are low-country Sinhalese governed by the general law. The gift was made by deed No. 4417 dated 12 March 2019, attested by a notary public and registered in the Kalutara land registry, and the deed states that the gift is absolute and irrevocable. The donee took possession, and the donor went on living in an annexe of the same house as the deed allowed him to. In June 2023 the donee assaulted the donor during a quarrel over the property, causing injuries for which the donor was treated in hospital, and then locked the annexe and put the donor out of the premises; the donor has since been living with a daughter. The donee does not deny the assault or the eviction and refuses to reconvey the property. The donor consults you in October 2023 and asks whether he can get the property back.

### Legal issues

- ISS-M039-Q01-01: Whether a deed of gift that declares itself absolute and irrevocable can be revoked at all, notwithstanding those words.
- ISS-M039-Q01-02: If it can be revoked, on what ground, by whom, against whom and by what mode revocation must be effected, and whether the donor can revoke by his own act.
- ISS-M039-Q01-03: Whether the statutory time limits for filing the revocation action are satisfied on the date of execution of the deed and the date the cause of action arose.

### Draft answer

Issue: Whether a deed of gift which states on its face that it is absolute and irrevocable can nevertheless be revoked, and if so on what ground, by whom and in what manner; and whether the donor here is within the time allowed for seeking revocation of the 2019 deed after the donee's conduct in June 2023.

Rule: The words "absolute and irrevocable" in the deed do not put the gift beyond recall. Section 2 of the Revocation of Irrevocable Deeds of Gift on the Ground of Gross Ingratitude Act, No. 5 of 2017 provides that an irrevocable deed of gift may be revoked on the ground of gross ingratitude, only on an order made by a competent court, in an action filed by the donor against the donee. Three limits follow from the section itself: gross ingratitude is the only ground; an order of a competent court is the only mode, so the donor cannot revoke by his own act or by executing a deed of revocation; and the action lies at the suit of the donor against the donee. Section 3 adds that the court shall not entertain such an action unless it is filed within ten years from the date of execution of the deed of gift and within two years from the date on which the cause of action arose, and both limits must be satisfied. Under section 4(1) the plaintiff must file with the plaint an application for registration of the action as a lis pendens, addressed to the Registrar of Lands of the district in which the deed is registered or, where it is unregistered, in which the land lies. Under section 5 the action is proceeded with and regulated by the procedure of the Civil Procedure Code. The Act does not define gross ingratitude, so whether particular conduct amounts to it is for the court to decide on the evidence.

Application: The deed of gift of Kahatagahawatta was executed on 12 March 2019 and declares the gift absolute and irrevocable. That wording is no answer to a claim under section 2. The donor, Wimalasena Perera, is the proper plaintiff and the donee, his nephew Chaminda Perera, the proper defendant. The conduct relied on is the assault in June 2023 and the donor's eviction from the annexe of the very house he gave away, neither of which the donee denies; that is the material the donor would place before the court as gross ingratitude, though the finding is for the court and cannot be assumed. On the dates, an action filed in or shortly after October 2023 is within ten years of 12 March 2019 and within two years of the June 2023 conduct, so section 3 does not shut the donor out. Because the land is registered in the Kalutara land registry, the lis pendens application under section 4(1) goes to the Registrar of Lands of that district and must be filed with the plaint, and the action proceeds under the Civil Procedure Code. What the donor cannot do is execute a deed cancelling the gift and treat the property as his own again; nothing short of a court order will undo the gift.

Conclusion: Yes, it could be revoked, but only in one way. Despite the recital that it is absolute and irrevocable, the deed can be revoked on the ground of gross ingratitude by order of a competent court in an action filed by the donor against the donee under section 2 of Act No. 5 of 2017, and on these dates such an action is within both the ten-year and the two-year limits in section 3. The donor should be advised to sue promptly, filing the lis pendens application with the plaint; whether the donee's conduct amounts to gross ingratitude is a matter for the court on the evidence, and the donor has no power to revoke the deed himself.
