## Original background

A Deed of Gift States that it is absolute and irrevocable.

## M080-Q01 (enrich, hop count 2)

### Question

Could it be revoked?

### Enriched background

By a Deed of Gift bearing No. 2418 dated 12 March 2018, attested by a notary public at Kadawatha, Wimalasena Perera, a retired schoolmaster of Kadawatha, gifted to his nephew Chandana Perera the land and two-storeyed dwelling house called Sirisara Watta, eighteen perches in extent, situated at Kadawatha in the District of Gampaha. The deed was registered in the Gampaha land registry on 3 April 2018. The deed states that it is absolute and irrevocable, and no life interest, fiduciary interest or right of residence was reserved in it. After the gift Wimalasena, who is now seventy-four, continued to live in the house with his nephew's agreement. From February 2019 Chandana stopped supplying the donor with food and medicine, and in the course of a quarrel over the property he assaulted him. On 20 May 2019 he locked the donor out of the house and had the donor's belongings removed to a relative's home, where the donor has lived since. In October 2019 Wimalasena instructs an attorney-at-law that he wishes to have the gift undone. Chandana refuses to join in any cancellation of the deed and denies that he has done anything wrong.

### Legal issues

- ISS-M080-Q01-01: Whether a recital in a deed of gift that the gift is absolute and irrevocable prevents the gift from being revoked at all.
- ISS-M080-Q01-02: If revocation is possible, on what ground, at whose instance and by what mode it may be effected, and whether the donor can revoke by his own act.
- ISS-M080-Q01-03: Whether the revocation action is open in time, measured both from the execution of the deed and from the date the cause of action arose.
- ISS-M080-Q01-04: What must accompany the plaint in such an action and what procedure governs it.

### Draft answer

Issue: Whether a deed of gift which on its face declares the gift to be absolute and irrevocable can nevertheless be revoked; and if it can, on what ground, at whose instance, by what mode, within what time and with what procedural steps.

Rule: A statement in the deed that the gift is absolute and irrevocable prevents the donor from undoing the gift by his own act, but it does not put the gift beyond recall. Under the Revocation of Irrevocable Deeds of Gift on the Ground of Gross Ingratitude Act, No. 5 of 2017, s.2, an irrevocable deed of gift may be revoked on the ground of gross ingratitude, and only on an order made by a competent court in an action filed by the donor against the donee. Section 2 is exhaustive both as to the ground and as to the mode, so a unilateral deed of revocation by the donor will not do. Section 3 bars the court from entertaining such an action unless it is filed within ten years from the date of execution of the deed of gift and within two years from the date on which the cause of action arose; both limbs must be met. Section 4(1) requires the plaintiff to file with the plaint an application for registration of the action as a lis pendens, addressed to the Registrar of Lands of the district in which the deed sought to be revoked is registered or, where it is unregistered, of the district in which the land lies; Registrar of Lands is defined in s.6 by reference to the Registration of Documents Ordinance (Chapter 117), and s.11(6) of that Ordinance treats a duly registered action as pending from the time of registration. Section 5 applies the Civil Procedure Code to the action. The 2017 Act does not define gross ingratitude, so whether proved conduct reaches that standard is for the court on the evidence.

Application: The deed of 12 March 2018 recites that the gift is absolute and irrevocable, so Wimalasena cannot simply execute an instrument cancelling it, and the donee refuses to join in any cancellation. His only route is an action under s.2 against Chandana. The conduct relied on, withholding food and medicine from an elderly donor, an assault, and finally locking him out of the very house that was gifted, is pleaded as gross ingratitude; whether it is held to amount to gross ingratitude is a matter for the court, because the Act sets no definition. On the time bar in s.3, an action filed in or shortly after October 2019 is about one year and seven months after execution of the deed on 12 March 2018, well inside ten years, and about five months after the cause of action arose on 20 May 2019, well inside two years, so both limbs are satisfied. Because the deed was registered in the Gampaha land registry, the application for registration of lis pendens under s.4(1)(a) is addressed to the Registrar of Lands of the Gampaha district, in triplicate and substantially in the form set out in the Registration of Documents Ordinance, and it must be filed in court with the plaint; registration is what protects the donor if Chandana attempts to dispose of the land while the action is pending. The action then proceeds under the Civil Procedure Code by virtue of s.5.

Conclusion: Yes, the gift can be revoked, but not by the donor's own act and not on any ground he chooses. Notwithstanding the recital that it is absolute and irrevocable, the deed may be revoked only by an order of a competent court, on the single ground of gross ingratitude, in an action filed by the donor against the donee under s.2 of Act No. 5 of 2017. On these facts the action is within both limbs of the s.3 time bar, must be filed with a lis pendens application under s.4 addressed to the Registrar of Lands, Gampaha, and is prosecuted under the Civil Procedure Code under s.5. Whether relief is in fact granted depends on the court accepting that the donee's proved conduct amounts to gross ingratitude, a question the Act leaves open.
