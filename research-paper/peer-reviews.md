# Peer review notes

Review:
This paper studies whether statutory structure can improve retrieval of all legally indispensable provisions needed to answer Sri Lankan conveyancing questions. The authors construct a corpus of 52 principal enactments and 60 amending Acts, containing 4,557 sections and 6,346 typed statutory relationships, and evaluate seven retrieval approaches ranging from BM25 and dense retrieval to hierarchical and structure-aware retrieval. The benchmark contains 50 questions from 40 legal matters derived from Sri Lanka Law College examinations. Importantly, the proposed evaluation metric, complete-indispensable recall, requires retrieval of every indispensable provision rather than giving credit for retrieving only part of the relevant legal bundle. I find the problem well motivated. The paper makes a convincing argument that provision-level recall can be misleading in legal retrieval because an answer may be incomplete even when several relevant sections have been retrieved. The distinction among indispensable, supporting, and background provisions is especially useful. The benchmark also focuses on a genuinely low-resource legal setting, which fits the workshop well. The benchmark construction process is clearly explained, including matter-level grouping, temporal information, provenance, and the planned three-attorney validation procedure. The experimental comparison is another strength. The authors compare lexical, dense, hybrid, reranked, hierarchical, and graph-expanded retrieval under the same evaluation framework. The main result is interesting because the structure-aware system does not outperform the strong hybrid baseline: Hybrid RRF obtains C@20 = 0.344, while the hierarchical bundle system obtains 0.312, and the reported paired intervals do not establish an advantage for the structure-aware method. The paper further observes that no evaluated system completes questions requiring four or more indispensable sections. This is a useful negative result rather than an attempt to overstate an improvement. The main concern is the reliability of the benchmark gold labels. The indispensable provisions were produced and independently checked by agents, but no attorney ratings had been collected at submission time. Therefore, all reported results are measurements against proposed rather than legally validated gold labels. This is particularly important because distinguishing an indispensable legal authority from a merely supporting one is central to both the dataset and the principal metric. The authors are appropriately transparent about this limitation and provide a sensible three-attorney validation protocol, but completing at least part of this validation would substantially strengthen the empirical conclusions. The benchmark statistics explicitly report “lawyer approved 0.” A second limitation is the small evaluation set. The test set contains only 40 questions from 32 matters, resulting in relatively wide confidence intervals. Consequently, the paper can support the conclusion that the tested structure-aware implementation has not demonstrated an improvement, but stronger general claims about statutory structure would be premature. Third, the structure representation is relatively limited. Edges are derived largely from cue words, explicit cross-references, term-definition matching, and amendment relationships. The error analysis itself shows that only two of 114 indispensable sections missed by the structure-aware system were reachable by a single typed edge from retrieved seeds. This is an insightful finding, but it also means the experiment primarily demonstrates the limitation of this explicit statutory graph rather than establishing that richer forms of legal structure or learned dependency representations are ineffective. Finally, some retrieval components are relatively generic for this legal domain. The MS MARCO cross-encoder substantially hurts performance, with MRR dropping from 0.543 for hybrid RRF to 0.223 after reranking. Evaluation with a stronger legal-domain retriever/reranked, query expansion, or learned relation model would help separate limitations of statutory structure from limitations of the retrieval models used.
Strengths: The paper addresses an important and underexplored low-resource legal NLP problem; introduces a meaningful complete-bundle retrieval metric; provides a carefully constructed statute-level corpus with typed relationships; compares several retrieval paradigms under matched conditions; reports negative findings clearly rather than overstating results; and provides useful ablations, confidence intervals, reproducibility information, and error analysis.
Weaknesses: The principal benchmark labels have not yet been validated by legal experts; the test set is small; the corpus excludes a substantial number of mixed/case-law and missing-authority questions; the graph structure is based on relatively simple heuristic relations; and therefore, the negative conclusion should remain explicitly limited to the particular structure representation and retrieval approach evaluated. The authors themselves acknowledge several of these limitations, including pending gold validation, corpus coverage, heuristic edge typing, approximate temporal filtering, and the absence of answer-generation evaluation.
Rating: 6: Marginally above acceptance threshold
Confidence: 4: The reviewer is confident but not absolutely certain that the evaluation is correct
