1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
================================================================================

Draftly is a lawyer-in-the-loop workflow platform for Sri Lankan conveyancing
practice. The first prototype targets matters under the title-registration
regime (Registration of Title Act, No. 21 of 1998, the "Bim Saviya"
programme), covering examination of title, drafting, execution, and
attestation, with the Form 8 transfer as the first end-to-end instrument. A
litigation workflow track is planned as a later extension of the same matter
and workflow foundation but is out of scope for the initial build.

The system takes an unstructured bundle of property and identity documents,
extracts candidate facts through OCR and layout-aware field extraction,
requires a qualified lawyer to verify or correct every fact before it is
relied on, runs deterministic consistency checks across the verified record,
answers legal questions with citations grounded in a curated statutory and
case-law corpus, and generates draft instruments from prescribed forms
populated only with verified facts. Every step is logged to an audit trail.

This report assesses feasibility across seven dimensions: technical,
operational, economic, legal and regulatory, schedule, risk, and
alternatives. The finding in each case is that the project is feasible,
provided two conditions the brief itself already anticipates are respected:
that language-model use stays confined to bounded, citation-checked tasks
with explicit abstention, and that no automated output reaches a client
without a lawyer's verification and approval.

The strongest evidence for technical feasibility is not the brief's
description of the target architecture but the state of the repository
itself. The retrieval engine, the highest-risk component, already exists as
working code: a normalized statutory and case-law corpus, a BM25-based
lexical retrieval index with a citation graph, a Streamlit demonstration
application, and an evaluation harness with recorded metrics across several
runs. The remainder of the platform, the Next.js matter workspace, the
FastAPI service layer beyond the retrieval engine, the PostgreSQL persistence
layer, and the Tiptap-based draft editor, is planned but not yet built in
this codebase. That gap is normal for a project at this stage, but it means
the majority of implementation effort still lies ahead, concentrated in the
interface and integration tracks rather than in the corpus and retrieval work
already validated.

The recommendation is to proceed, with the staged plan the brief already
lays out: freeze the shared matter-record contracts early, build the
interface against mock data in parallel with engine hardening, and treat the
first end-to-end path (intake through an approved, exported Form 8 draft) as
the milestone that de-risks everything after it.

2. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
================================================================================

2.1 What Draftly is

Draftly is a web-based platform intended for practising lawyers and notaries
who handle land and title work, together with their supporting staff. It is
built around a "matter": a single conveyancing transaction that accumulates
documents, verified facts, consistency checks, legal question-and-answer
sessions, and drafts as it moves toward completion. The platform supports two
workflow tracks. Conveyancing is the first prototype. Litigation is described
in the brief as a later extension of the same underlying matter and workflow
model, not a parallel product.

The defining design choice, stated directly in the brief, is what Draftly
deliberately is not: it is not a form-filling PDF generator, and it is not a
legal search engine. A prescribed statutory form is only the final output
layer. Every draft the system produces has to be traceable back to a
verified fact and to the document that fact came from.

2.2 The problem it addresses

Conveyancing practice in Sri Lanka is document-heavy and almost entirely
manual. A single transfer matter can involve deeds, the current title
certificate, cadastral and survey material, local-authority assessment
records, and identity documents, often spanning decades and, per the brief,
four coexisting registration regimes. Examination of title depends on every
one of those documents agreeing on the same lot number, extent, boundary, and
party names. A mismatch that goes unnoticed can invalidate an instrument or
expose the practitioner to liability.

The brief identifies five specific difficulties in the current process:

  - Manual reconciliation of particulars across a document bundle, done by
    eye, slow and error-prone, with the burden falling on junior staff.
  - Fragmented sources of law: statutes, amendments, gazettes, prescribed
    forms, and case law are scattered, and existing local research tools
    support general search rather than guidance tied to a specific step of a
    specific matter.
  - Transcription risk when particulars are re-typed from source documents
    into instruments, especially where prescribed statutory wording must be
    reproduced exactly.
  - No systematic record of what was verified, by whom, or against what
    evidence, which weakens both internal quality control and the
    defensibility of the finished work.
  - The unreliability of general-purpose AI tools in this setting: fluent
    answers with fabricated citations are a disqualifying failure mode in
    legal work, not a minor defect.

2.3 Value to a practice

The brief frames the expected value as faster and more reliable title
examination, fewer transcription and consistency errors, citation-grounded
answers to legal questions, and a defensible record of who verified and
approved each fact and document. None of this replaces professional
judgment. Every consequential action, verification, approval, waiver, or
export, stays with a qualified lawyer; the platform's job is to remove
clerical burden, not decision-making authority.

3. PROJECT OBJECTIVES AND SCOPE
================================================================================

3.1 Objectives

The brief sets nine objectives for the project:

  1. Design and implement a web-based platform in which a matter progresses
     from document intake to an approved, exportable draft under continuous
     lawyer supervision.
  2. Automate extraction of structured particulars from Sinhala and English
     conveyancing documents using OCR, document classification, and
     layout-aware field extraction, with every extracted value carrying its
     source location.
  3. Provide a verification workspace where extracted facts are reviewed
     beside their source evidence and moved through explicit verification
     states before use.
  4. Implement deterministic consistency checks over the verified matter
     record, including cross-matching the deed schedule, survey plan, and
     municipal assessment records, producing missing-document checklists and
     red flags.
  5. Provide grounded legal question answering over a curated corpus of Sri
     Lankan statutes, gazettes, and case law, where every claim carries a
     citation and the system abstains when authority is insufficient.
  6. Generate draft instruments from prescribed statutory forms, starting
     with the Form 8 transfer, populated only from verified facts.
  7. Maintain versioned drafts, approval gates, export to standard office
     formats, and a complete audit trail.
  8. Provide the interface in English and Sinhala on the demonstrated
     workflow path.
  9. Evaluate extraction, checking, retrieval, and drafting quality against a
     lawyer-verified evaluation set before any capability is presented as
     reliable.

3.2 Scope

The initial system is bounded to matters under the title-registration regime
(Registration of Title Act, No. 21 of 1998), covering examination of title,
drafting, execution, and attestation, with Form 8 as the first end-to-end
instrument.

The brief defines a single user role in detail, the lawyer or notary, who is
able to create and manage matters, upload documents and monitor their
processing state, review and verify or correct extracted facts, run and
resolve consistency checks and red flags, follow guided workflow steps with
visible statutory grounding, ask matter-aware legal questions and inspect
claim-level evidence, generate and edit drafts from approved templates,
compare and restore versions, approve drafts, export approved drafts, and
inspect the full audit trail.

Explicitly out of scope for the initial build:

  - The registration function of notarial practice, historical title-chain
    reconstruction, and pedigree analysis.
  - Conveyancing regimes other than title registration. Deed registration,
    condominium, and special-area regimes appear in the interface but are
    marked as not yet supported.
  - The litigation workflow track.
  - Document forms and classes explicitly excluded from the initial
    extraction pipeline.

3.3 Deliverables

The brief lists the following as the project's main outputs: the web
application itself (matter management, intake, verification, checks, guided
workflow, question answering, draft editing with version history, approval
gates, export); the bilingual document-processing pipeline; the curated
statutory and case-law corpus with its retrieval engine and citation graph;
the Form 8 draft-generation module with locked prescribed wording and export
to standard office formats; the bilingual interface on the demonstrated
path; a lawyer-verified evaluation set with evaluation reports; and the
supporting technical documentation, user documentation, testing reports,
deployment instructions, and source code.

4. TECHNICAL FEASIBILITY
================================================================================

4.1 Proposed architecture

The target architecture described in the brief has five parts: a Next.js
(React, TypeScript) web application covering the matter workspace,
verification interface, and draft editor; a FastAPI (Python) service layer
exposing the document pipeline and retrieval engine over typed HTTP
interfaces; a PostgreSQL store for matters, facts, drafts, versions, and
audit records; a document-processing pipeline for OCR, classification, and
field extraction with source spans; and a deterministic retrieval engine over
a curated statutory and case-law corpus, with bounded, validated
language-model passes for constrained enrichment tasks.

4.2 Proposed stack, by layer

  Frontend: Next.js with TypeScript, Tailwind CSS, an accessible component
  library, and the Tiptap rich-text editor. Tiptap's schema validation is the
  mechanism that locks prescribed statutory wording while leaving schedules
  and particulars editable, and lets facts be embedded as typed nodes that
  refuse unverified content.

  Backend: FastAPI, chosen because the data-science components are already
  Python, so a Python web framework serves the engine directly rather than
  requiring a second runtime.

  Database: PostgreSQL for matters, facts, documents, versions, and audit
  records. SQLite full-text indexes handle the statutory corpus inside the
  retrieval engine specifically.

  Document processing: a tiered OCR strategy, local open-source engines for
  routine material and a cloud document-understanding service for difficult
  scans, with every result subject to manual verification. Self-hosted OCR
  is the preferred route for real client documents on privacy grounds.

  Retrieval: a deterministic, rule-based engine using BM25 lexical ranking,
  a citation graph linking cases to statutory sections, and topic-partitioned
  indexes. Bounded large-language-model passes are used only for constrained
  tasks with validated outputs, following retrieval-augmented generation
  principles with claim-level citation checking and explicit abstention.

  Supporting tooling: Git and GitHub for version control and CI, Vercel for
  frontend preview deployments, Playwright and axe-core for interface and
  accessibility testing, Conventional Commits and code review for change
  control.

The brief is explicit that these selections may change as development
proceeds; the architecture, not the specific vendor, is the commitment.

4.3 Current implementation status (repository evidence)

The distinction between the target architecture above and what the
repository actually contains today is material to a feasibility judgment, so
it is stated here directly rather than left implicit.

What exists now is the research and retrieval track, not the full platform.
The src/draftly/retrieval package (corpus.py, index.py, search.py,
answering.py, graph.py, embeddings.py, section_parser.py, evaluation.py,
qa_evaluation.py, question_analysis.py) implements the corpus, the BM25
index, the citation graph, and a Gemini-backed answering module, exposed
through a Streamlit demonstration at apps/statute-retrieval/app.py rather
than through FastAPI and Next.js. pyproject.toml confirms the stack this
code actually uses: docling and onnxruntime for document parsing and local
model inference, google-genai for the bounded language-model passes,
google-cloud-documentai for the cloud OCR tier, and streamlit for the demo
interface. (google-cloud-speech and google-cloud-storage are also declared
dependencies; their specific role was not evident from the code reviewed for
this report and is worth confirming with the team.)

The README describes the demo's current coverage precisely: 57 statutes and
18 amendments indexed, no case law yet, Gemini answers limited to retrieved
sections with each retained claim checked against its citations, and a
retrieval evaluation running against ten unverified development labels. A
larger question set exists (18 exam questions and 73 subquestions in
src/questions.md) but the README states directly that it does not report
legal correctness without lawyer gold labels.

The data and evaluation layers back this up. data/legal-sources holds the
normalized corpus (library, library-markdown, manifests, topics,
curriculum). evaluation/ contains a gold-label file (retrieval-gold.csv), an
answer-evaluation rubric, a qa-agent baseline/upgraded comparison with
recorded metrics, and several named evaluation runs (for example
caselaw-ablation-v1, statute-qa-v2, statute-qa-v2-final) each with its own
config, metrics, and raw results. notebooks/ contains a BM25 baseline, a
case-law extraction validation notebook, a corpus exploratory-analysis
notebook, and a headnote rule-recovery notebook. The most recent commits on
the repository (as of this report) add a draft retrieval evaluation gold
set specifically for conveyancing information retrieval, which lines up
with the brief's claim that the hardest components have already been
validated through working prototypes.

What is not yet present: the Next.js matter workspace, the FastAPI service
layer for anything beyond the retrieval engine, PostgreSQL persistence for
matters and facts, the Tiptap draft editor with schema-locked prescribed
wording, the deterministic consistency-check engine over verified facts, and
the Form 8 template itself. These map to stages 2 through 7 of the brief's
own staged plan (section 8 below) and represent the larger share of
remaining engineering work. This is not a sign of infeasibility; the brief's
own logic, that the interface can be built against mock data without waiting
for the backend, is precisely designed to absorb this gap without stalling
the schedule. It is, however, the single fact a client or mentor reviewing
this report should not miss: the corpus and retrieval work is validated, the
product around it is still to be built.

4.4 Integration points

The system has five points where independently developed components meet,
and each is a place where a contract has to be frozen and tested rather than
assumed:

  - Frontend to backend, over typed HTTP interfaces exposed by FastAPI.
  - Backend to database, PostgreSQL for the matter record, SQLite FTS
    specifically for the statutory corpus inside the retrieval engine.
  - Backend to the document pipeline, where OCR and extraction results carry
    source spans and confidence values into the fact-verification workspace.
  - Backend to the retrieval engine, where a matter-aware question is
    answered against the corpus and returned with citations that resolve
    against it.
  - Editor to backend, where the Tiptap schema enforces, structurally rather
    than by convention, that no unverified fact can enter a draft.

4.5 Technical risks

Three risks are specific to this architecture rather than generic to
software projects. Sinhala OCR quality on old, degraded scans is the least
predictable element in the pipeline; the brief's answer is to route
difficult documents through progressively stronger engines and fall back to
manual entry through the same verification workflow when extraction fails,
so the system degrades rather than breaks. Language-model reliability is a
second risk that is disqualifying if unmanaged, since fabricated citations
are unacceptable in legal work; the mitigation is architectural, models are
confined to bounded tasks with closed choices and validated outputs, every
claim must cite resolvable authority, and the system abstains when authority
is insufficient. Third, components built in parallel by three developers
(document processing, retrieval and corpus, interface and templates) can
fail to integrate cleanly; the brief's mitigation is to freeze shared typed
contracts first and build the interface against mock data shaped like the
engine's real output before wiring the two together. Section 9 covers these
and the remaining risk categories in full.

4.6 Conclusion on technical feasibility

Each core framework in the proposed stack, Next.js, FastAPI, PostgreSQL, and
Tiptap, is mature and well documented, and the components interoperate over
standard typed HTTP interfaces. More importantly, the highest-risk piece of
the system, grounded retrieval over a Sri Lankan legal corpus, is not a
paper design; it runs today, against a real corpus, with recorded evaluation
metrics. On that basis, and taking the current repository state as evidence
rather than the brief's description alone, the project is technically
feasible, with the remaining work concentrated in the platform layer around
the already-validated engine.

5. OPERATIONAL FEASIBILITY
================================================================================

5.1 Fit to real conveyancing practice

The platform is designed around the profession's existing workflow rather
than a generic document-management pattern. The four notarial functions it
targets (examination of title, drafting, execution, attestation) are the
actual stages of a title-registration matter, and the guided workflow steps
are tied to the statutory provisions that govern each one. The brief states
this design choice explicitly: the interface reports what is missing rather
than imposing a generic checklist on the practitioner.

5.2 The lawyer-in-the-loop workflow

The operating principle running through every stage is that automation
proposes and a lawyer disposes. OCR and field extraction produce candidate
facts, not accepted ones; a fact only becomes usable once a lawyer verifies
or corrects it against its source evidence. Consistency checks surface
mismatches and missing documents but do not resolve them unilaterally.
Legal question answering cites its evidence and abstains rather than
guessing when the corpus does not support an answer. Draft generation pulls
only from verified facts and locked prescribed wording, and a draft still
requires lawyer approval before export. The audit trail records who did
what, and when, across all of it.

This is what separates Draftly from a form-filling tool or a legal search
engine in practice, not just in description: at no point does the system's
output become a client-facing instrument without a named, accountable
professional having reviewed it.

5.3 Adoption considerations

The brief's own risk analysis names user adoption directly: practitioners
may distrust automated assistance, or may find a new workflow more
burdensome than the manual process it replaces, particularly given how much
professional liability rides on title examination being right. The
mitigations offered are that the workflow mirrors the profession's own
process as described by the domain-expert mentor, that every automated
output is traceable to evidence and requires explicit approval, and that a
bilingual interface removes a language barrier that would otherwise limit
who on a practice's staff can use the system.

One point worth flagging for the team: the brief's evidence for workflow fit
comes from a single domain-expert mentor, described as a senior practitioner
and law lecturer, rather than from testing across multiple independent
practices. That is a reasonable and common way to ground a capstone-scale
project, but it means workflow validation with a second or third
practitioner, outside the mentor relationship, would meaningfully strengthen
the operational feasibility case before any claim of general practice fit is
made. This is not stated as a gap in the brief; it is an assumption this
report is flagging based on what the brief does and does not describe about
its validation process.

5.4 Operational fit summary

Given that every consequential action remains with a qualified lawyer, that
the workflow is built from and validated against actual notarial practice
rather than assumed, and that the bilingual interface addresses a real
adoption barrier rather than a hypothetical one, the platform is
operationally feasible for the scope it targets. Its operational case is
strongest for the mentor's own practice pattern and should be treated as
provisional, not proven, for the broader profession until tested more
widely.

6. ECONOMIC AND FINANCIAL FEASIBILITY
================================================================================

6.1 Development costs

The brief's cost structure is dominated by free and open-source components.
Development hardware is existing personal computers; no GPU is required
because model inference runs as an external service and the retrieval core
is deterministic. Next.js, FastAPI, PostgreSQL, Tiptap's core, Playwright,
and the supporting toolchain are open source. Cloud OCR runs on a free-tier
or trial allocation with a capped page budget, with local open-source OCR as
the default route. Language-model usage runs on free developer tiers, and
the client is provider-agnostic so a provider can be swapped without code
changes. Frontend preview hosting and version control and CI both run on
free tiers.

6.2 Operational costs

Operational costs during the project period stay within free tiers, per the
brief. A production deployment beyond the project would introduce modest
recurring costs, managed hosting and database services, document storage,
and paid OCR or inference tiers if volume grows. The brief characterizes
this as "the order of a small monthly subscription" without stating a
figure. A domain name and TLS certificate are noted as minor optional costs.

Assumption flagged for the team: no specific monthly or annual cost estimate
is given anywhere in the source material for a post-project production
deployment. "Small monthly subscription" is a qualitative claim, not a
budget. Before any production rollout is costed for a real practice, this
report recommends getting concrete quotes for managed PostgreSQL hosting,
document storage at expected matter volumes, and paid-tier OCR and
inference pricing, since these are the line items most likely to scale with
adoption.

6.3 Expected benefits

The brief lists five categories of benefit: reduced professional time per
matter for title examination, document reconciliation, and drafting; fewer
transcription and consistency errors, which reduces rework and professional
risk; faster location of governing provisions through grounded, step-aware
retrieval; a defensible verification and audit record that improves quality
control within a practice; and a reusable structured corpus and
matter-record foundation that lets further regimes and the litigation track
be added at marginal cost rather than from scratch.

6.4 Conclusion on financial feasibility

None of these benefits are quantified in the source material with a rupee
figure or a time-saved estimate, so a formal cost-benefit ratio cannot be
computed from what is available. What can be said with confidence is
directional: development cost is close to zero because it draws on
resources the team already has and free service tiers, while the process
being addressed, title examination and drafting on billable professional
time, is expensive by nature and carries real liability exposure when it
goes wrong. On that basis the brief's conclusion, that the financial
benefits justify the costs, holds. A quantified benefit estimate (hours
saved per matter, error-rate reduction) would strengthen this section
considerably and is recommended as a follow-up once the evaluation set
produces real numbers.

7. LEGAL AND REGULATORY FEASIBILITY
================================================================================

7.1 Data protection

The brief designs data handling for compliance with the Personal Data
Protection Act, No. 9 of 2022: data minimisation, purpose limitation,
role-restricted access, security safeguards, and retention controls. Real
client data is kept out of development artefacts entirely. This aligns with
the repository-level instruction (CLAUDE.md) that data/raw contains private
legal documents that must not be published, copied into public docs, or
represented in demo material with real names, NICs, or addresses without
explicit anonymization approval. The four sample matter bundles currently
present under data/raw exist for internal pipeline testing; this report does
not reproduce their contents, consistent with that instruction.

7.2 Confidentiality

The brief addresses legal-practice confidentiality obligations through
access control, audit trails, and a stated preference for self-hosted
processing of real client documents rather than routing them through a
third-party cloud OCR service by default.

7.3 Sources of law

The statutory corpus is assembled from official and public sources; case
law comes from public records via official and publicly accessible
repositories. The brief flags one specific limitation: editorial material
from commercial law reports, headnotes in particular, carries third-party
copyright and is used only internally for research, never republished. This
matters directly for the corpus work already in the repository
(notebooks/04_headnote_rule_recovery.ipynb), and the same restriction should
apply to any headnote-derived content that reaches a public demo or an
exported client draft.

7.4 Prescribed forms and statutory wording

Prescribed wording from the governing statute and gazetted forms is
reproduced exactly and locked against editing in the platform's design. The
brief is careful about what this does and does not mean legally: drafting
legal wording in templates remains a human responsibility, and a generated
draft is an instrument prepared for and approved by a qualified
professional. On this basis the brief argues the system does not practise
law or offer legal advice to the public, since no output reaches a client
without that professional approval step.

7.5 Licensing

The brief states that all selected frameworks, libraries, and fonts carry
permissive open-source licences compatible with the project's use. This
report has not independently audited a dependency license file for every
package in pyproject.toml or a future frontend package.json; before any
public release, a dependency license scan is a reasonable, low-cost step to
confirm this claim holds in practice rather than by design intent.

7.6 Electronic documents

Exported instruments follow existing conveyancing practice: printed,
executed, and attested conventionally. Because of that, the brief notes the
system does not need to resolve the electronic-execution questions that
surround instruments excluded from the Electronic Transactions Act, No. 19
of 2006; the platform's electronic records serve internal workflow and
audit purposes rather than substituting for physical execution.

7.7 Liability for accuracy

The brief treats the verification architecture itself, evidence-side
review, explicit verification states, deterministic checks, citation gates,
abstention, and mandatory lawyer approval before export, as the mitigation
for liability risk arising from inaccurate automated output. This is a
reasonable design position: liability risk is addressed structurally rather
than through a disclaimer.

7.8 Gap flagged for the team

The brief's legal-feasibility discussion is scoped to data protection,
confidentiality, copyright in source material, and the standing of the
generated draft. It does not address professional-conduct or regulatory
requirements specific to notarial practice itself, such as any rules a
notary's supervising body may impose on the use of software tools in
preparing instruments for attestation. Nothing in the repository or the
brief speaks to this, so this report does not assert a position on it. It is
flagged here as a question worth putting directly to the mentor, given the
mentor's standing as a senior practitioner, before the platform is used on
any real matter.

8. SCHEDULE FEASIBILITY
================================================================================

8.1 Staged plan

The brief organizes the project into seven ordered stages rather than a
dated calendar:

  1. Requirement gathering, domain study, and feasibility analysis. Marked
     complete in the brief: mentor-guided domain model, corpus acquisition,
     and interface specification.
  2. Contracts and foundations: the shared matter-record and step-definition
     contracts that all three workstreams build against.
  3. Engine and content track: retrieval engine hardening, evaluation sets,
     and the rule and check catalogue, run in parallel with the interface
     track.
  4. Interface track: the full workspace built against typed mock data,
     matched to the approved interface specification.
  5. Integration: the interface wired to the service layer, producing the
     first end-to-end demonstrable path, matter creation through verified
     facts, checks, grounded answers, and an approved exported draft.
  6. Completion of remaining workflows, bilingual polish, evaluation
     against lawyer-verified labels, and hardening.
  7. Testing, user evaluation with the mentor, documentation, and final
     reporting.

8.2 Parallelism and risk buffering

Three developers divide the work into three parallel workstreams: document
processing and extraction; data, corpus, and retrieval engine; and
templates, output, and the lawyer-facing interface. The brief's schedule
logic rests on two structural choices: the interface can be built against
mock data without waiting for the backend to be finished, and stage 5
defines a minimal demonstrable product early, which protects the schedule
against overruns in the later, harder stages (bilingual polish, full
evaluation, hardening).

8.3 Gap flagged for the team

The source material gives ordering and parallelism but no calendar dates,
durations, or an end date for any stage. This report does not invent one.
Before this schedule can be tracked against an actual deadline (an academic
term boundary, a mentor review date, or a submission date), the team should
attach specific dates to each of the seven stages, ideally with the same
granularity already used for stage 1 ("complete"). Given that the
repository's own commit history shows active work on stage 3 (corpus
normalization, citation graph, retrieval evaluation gold set) as of this
report's date, stage 3 appears underway rather than pending, which is worth
reconciling explicitly against the brief's stage descriptions.

8.4 Conclusion on schedule feasibility

The staged structure is sound: it front-loads the highest-uncertainty
technical work (already partly done, per section 4.3), defines an early
minimal demonstrable product, and uses parallel workstreams behind frozen
contracts to avoid one team member blocking another. Whether the schedule
fits an actual deadline cannot be assessed without the calendar detail
flagged above.

================================================================================
10. ALTERNATIVES CONSIDERED
================================================================================

10.1 Continue the manual process

The default alternative is no system at all: continued manual reconciliation
of document bundles and manual drafting. The brief's own needs analysis
(section 2.3 of this report) argues against this on the grounds that manual
reconciliation is slow and error-prone, sources of law are fragmented and
hard to search step-by-step, transcription is a standing source of risk, and
there is no systematic verification record. This alternative costs nothing
to adopt and requires no feasibility discussion, but it also does not
address any of the four problems the brief identifies.

10.2 Existing Sri Lankan legal research platforms

LawLanka and LawCeylon provide commercial access to Sri Lankan legislation
and law reports; Paralegal.lk and AI PAZZ offer search-oriented assistance
over local legal content. The brief's assessment is that these serve legal
research, retrieval of legislation and case law by query, and do nothing
else: they do not manage matters, extract or verify facts from client
documents, check cross-document consistency, or generate instruments.
Draftly's retrieval component covers similar ground to these tools, but as
one piece inside a verified drafting workflow rather than as the product
itself. Adopting one of these platforms instead of building Draftly would
leave every other problem in section 2.3 unaddressed.

10.3 International AI legal platforms

Harvey is named in the brief as a prominent example of an AI-assisted legal
workspace combining document analysis, drafting assistance, and workflow
features for large law firms. The brief credits it with demonstrating that
the calm, matter-centred professional workspace pattern works in practice,
which is part of why Draftly's own interface follows a similar shape. Its
limitation for this context is direct: it does not address Sri Lankan law,
Sinhala-language documents, local registration regimes, or the prescribed
forms that govern local notarial practice, and it is commercially positioned
toward large international firms rather than local practitioners. Adapting
or licensing a platform like this was not a viable alternative given that
gap.

10.4 General-purpose consumer language models

Consumer chatbots built on large language models can summarise and draft
text, but the brief is blunt about why this is not a workable alternative
for this domain: they lack access to the governing local sources, provide no
provenance for their claims, and hallucinate authority. They offer no matter
record, no verification workflow, and no audit trail. Given that the brief
treats fabricated citations as a disqualifying failure mode in legal work,
this alternative was set aside rather than adopted in any form.

10.5 Why the proposed approach was chosen

The brief's own conclusion is that no existing system combines, for the Sri
Lankan context, bilingual document extraction with lawyer verification,
deterministic title-consistency checks, grounded question answering over
local statutes and case law, and template-constrained generation of
prescribed instruments. That combination, not any single piece of it, is
what the alternatives above each fail to provide, and it is the gap Draftly
is built to fill.

================================================================================
11. CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATION
================================================================================

Across the seven dimensions examined in this report, technical, operational,
economic, legal and regulatory, schedule, risk, and alternatives, the
project holds up as feasible. The technical case is unusually well
supported for a project at this stage, since the hardest component,
grounded legal retrieval over a Sri Lankan corpus, already runs as code with
recorded evaluation metrics rather than existing only as a design on paper.
The operational case rests on a workflow built from and validated against
actual notarial practice, provided the current single-mentor validation is
broadened before any general claim of practice fit is made. The economic
case is directionally sound but not quantified: no cost-benefit figure
exists yet, only a reasonable argument that near-zero development cost
against an expensive, high-liability manual process should net favourably.
The legal and regulatory case is well handled for data protection,
confidentiality, and the standing of generated drafts, with one open
question, notarial professional-conduct requirements, that the brief does
not address and that this report recommends raising with the mentor
directly. The schedule is structurally sound but lacks calendar dates. The
risk register is thorough and each risk carries a concrete mitigation rather
than a general reassurance.

Recommendation: proceed with the project as scoped in the brief, following
its own staged plan. Three actions would strengthen the case further before
or during the next stage of work: attach calendar dates to the seven
schedule stages so progress can be tracked against a real deadline; obtain
concrete hosting, storage, and inference pricing to replace the current
qualitative cost estimate before any production deployment is planned; and
validate the workflow with a second practitioner outside the mentor
relationship once the interface reaches a demonstrable state, to test
whether the operational fit generalizes beyond the one practice it was
designed against.

None of these three actions block the next stage of work. They are
refinements to an already sound plan, not preconditions for starting it.
